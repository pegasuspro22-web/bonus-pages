Patient/Care Management

Q1Pulmonary embolism
On postoperative day one after a total knee replacement, a patient suddenly reports sharp chest pain and shortness of breath. Heart rate is 124/min and oxygen saturation is 88% on room air. What should the RN do first?

Why B is correct

Sudden pleuritic pain, tachycardia and hypoxemia after orthopedic surgery suggest pulmonary embolism, so oxygenation and immediate help come before any other step.

Why the others are wrong

A. Massaging a leg that may hold a clot can dislodge more emboli.

C. Waiting half an hour leaves a possibly life-threatening hypoxemia untreated.

D. An analgesic does not address the hypoxemia and may mask the progression of symptoms.

Q2Diabetic ketoacidosis
A patient in diabetic ketoacidosis is receiving a continuous insulin infusion. The latest serum potassium is 3.0 mEq/L. Which action should the RN take?

Why B is correct

Insulin drives potassium into cells, so starting or continuing it with a level below 3.3 mEq/L can cause dangerous arrhythmias until potassium is replaced.

Why the others are wrong

A. A faster insulin rate would lower serum potassium further and raise the risk of arrhythmia.

C. Delaying action allows a critically low potassium to fall further while insulin keeps running.

D. Bicarbonate shifts potassium into cells and is not the first correction for hypokalemia.

Q3Heparin infusion
A patient receiving a continuous heparin infusion has an activated partial thromboplastin time of 118 seconds, well above the therapeutic range. There is no visible bleeding. What should the RN do?

Why B is correct

A markedly prolonged aPTT means excess anticoagulation, so the infusion is paused and the dose is revised by the prescriber to prevent bleeding.

Why the others are wrong

A. Leaving a supratherapeutic infusion running for hours puts the patient at avoidable risk.

C. Vitamin K reverses warfarin, while protamine is the heparin antidote and is used only when ordered.

D. Absence of bleeding does not remove the risk; levels this high can precede hemorrhage.

Q4Chest tube
During repositioning, the tubing between a patient's chest tube and the drainage system separates. The patient is breathing comfortably. What is the priority action?

Why C is correct

The system must stay sealed or water-sealed to avoid a pneumothorax, so the open end is restored or submerged while help is called.

Why the others are wrong

A. Clamping can trap air and fluid and lead to a tension pneumothorax.

B. A taped dressing blocks air escape and can cause a tension pneumothorax.

D. An open tube lets air enter the pleural space and collapse the lung.

Q5Autonomic dysreflexia
A patient with a T4 spinal cord injury develops a pounding headache, flushing above the injury, and a blood pressure of 196/112 mm Hg. Which action should the RN take first?

Why A is correct

Autonomic dysreflexia is triggered by a noxious stimulus below the injury; sitting up lowers blood pressure and removing the trigger, often bladder or bowel, resolves it.

Why the others are wrong

B. Sedation leaves the triggering stimulus in place while the pressure stays dangerously high.

C. Lying flat would raise blood pressure in the head and worsen the crisis.

D. Heat on insensate skin can cause burns and may add to the noxious stimulus.

Q6Increased intracranial pressure
A patient with a traumatic brain injury has a rising intracranial pressure reading. Which positioning is most appropriate?

Why C is correct

Mild elevation with a midline head allows venous drainage from the brain, which lowers intracranial pressure.

Why the others are wrong

A. Neck flexion or rotation compresses the jugular veins and blocks drainage.

B. Head-down tilt raises intracranial pressure and risks herniation.

D. A flat position impairs venous outflow and can increase pressure inside the skull.

Q7Hypoglycemia
A diabetic patient is found drowsy and diaphoretic, with a glucose of 38 mg/dL. The patient cannot safely swallow and has a working IV line. What is the best action?

Why D is correct

A patient who cannot swallow safely needs IV dextrose (or glucagon if no access) because oral intake risks aspiration.

Why the others are wrong

A. Giving oral fluids to a drowsy patient who cannot swallow safely creates an aspiration risk.

B. Oral glucose is unsafe in a drowsy patient who cannot swallow.

C. A delay leaves severe hypoglycemia untreated and risks seizures or coma.

Q8Transfusion reaction
Fifteen minutes into a red blood cell transfusion, a patient develops chills, flank pain, and fever. What is the RN's first action?

Why B is correct

These signs point to an acute hemolytic reaction, so the blood is stopped immediately and access is kept open for emergency treatment.

Why the others are wrong

A. The remaining blood in the tubing can still deliver incompatible cells.

C. Continuing the blood exposes the patient to more incompatible cells.

D. Chills with flank pain and fever are warning signs, not a temperature effect.

Q9Parenteral nutrition
A patient's total parenteral nutrition bag runs out and the next bag has not arrived. What should the RN do?

Why A is correct

Abrupt cessation of concentrated dextrose can cause rebound hypoglycemia, so a dextrose solution bridges the gap.

Why the others are wrong

B. Stopping suddenly can drop the glucose rapidly in a patient adapted to high dextrose.

C. Saline keeps the line open but gives no glucose and can overload fluid.

D. This provides no dextrose and adds potassium without an order.

Q10Digoxin toxicity
A patient taking digoxin has nausea, new yellow-green halos in vision, and a potassium level of 3.1 mEq/L. The apical pulse is 54/min. What should the RN do?

Why B is correct

Low potassium increases digoxin toxicity; bradycardia, nausea and visual changes call for withholding the drug and prompt evaluation.

Why the others are wrong

A. Increasing the dose would deepen the toxicity and could cause fatal arrhythmias.

C. Symptoms with a low potassium indicate toxicity, regardless of a pulse above 50.

D. Food does not correct toxicity and delays the needed provider assessment.

Q11Unstageable pressure injury
The RN finds a sacral wound covered completely by dry, black, adherent eschar. The base cannot be seen. How should the wound be classified?

Why B is correct

Full-thickness loss obscured by slough or eschar is classified as unstageable until enough tissue is removed to expose the base.

Why the others are wrong

A. Deep tissue injury shows intact or blistered skin with persistent maroon or purple discoloration, not a covered full-thickness wound.

C. Stage 2 has partial thickness loss with a pink wound bed, never eschar.

D. Stage 1 shows intact skin with nonblanchable redness, not black necrotic tissue.

Q12Hypocalcemia after thyroidectomy
Six hours after a total thyroidectomy, a patient reports tingling around the mouth and fingertips. Tapping the facial nerve causes the cheek to twitch. What should the RN anticipate?

Why D is correct

Perioral tingling and a positive Chvostek sign suggest hypocalcemia from parathyroid injury, which is treated with IV calcium.

Why the others are wrong

A. Restricting calcium would worsen hypocalcemia.

B. This treats hyperkalemia and would not help a calcium deficit.

C. The findings reflect low calcium, and potassium would not treat them.

Q13Acute abdomen
Two days after bowel surgery, a patient has a rigid, board-like abdomen, rebound tenderness, fever, and absent bowel sounds. What is the best action?

Why B is correct

A rigid abdomen with rebound tenderness suggests peritonitis or anastomotic leak, an emergency needing urgent surgical evaluation.

Why the others are wrong

A. Stimulating the bowel with a perforation risk can worsen contamination.

C. Heat and analgesics can hide worsening peritonitis and delay the diagnosis.

D. Oral intake is unsafe, and delay allows sepsis to progress.

Q14Warfarin teaching
A patient newly prescribed warfarin asks about diet. Which statement by the RN is most accurate?

Why A is correct

Vitamin K affects warfarin action, so consistent intake keeps the INR stable instead of requiring complete avoidance.

Why the others are wrong

B. Eliminating vitamin K foods entirely is unnecessary and unhealthy.

C. A supplement would counteract the medication and weaken its benefit.

D. Large, variable amounts of vitamin K can lower the effect of warfarin.

Q15Heart failure teaching
A patient with heart failure is learning how to monitor for fluid retention at home. Which instruction is the most important?

Why A is correct

Daily weights detect fluid retention early, before edema or dyspnea become obvious.

Why the others are wrong

B. Self-adjusting diuretics can cause dehydration or electrolyte problems.

C. Weekly weighing and a high threshold would miss early decompensation.

D. Edema appears late, and weight changes are the earlier signal.

Q16Ischemic stroke
A patient arrives with sudden left-sided weakness and slurred speech. The family reports that the patient was last seen well about 90 minutes ago. What is the priority?

Why D is correct

The time of onset determines eligibility for thrombolytic therapy, so fast activation and the last-known-well time are the top priority.

Why the others are wrong

A. Rehabilitation is not an emergency priority and wastes critical time.

B. Delay shortens the narrow treatment window for stroke.

C. Oral intake is unsafe until swallowing is screened, and medication choice waits for imaging.

Q17Sepsis
A patient has fever, heart rate of 128/min, blood pressure of 84/48 mm Hg, and a suspected urinary source of infection. Which order of actions is most appropriate?

Why A is correct

Cultures before antibiotics identify the organism, while rapid antibiotics and fluids improve survival in septic shock.

Why the others are wrong

B. Treating only the fever ignores the infection and hypotension.

C. Delaying antibiotics increases mortality in sepsis.

D. Hypotension in sepsis needs prompt fluid resuscitation as prescribed.

Q18Tension pneumothorax
A patient on a ventilator suddenly has absent breath sounds on the left, tracheal deviation to the right, and falling blood pressure. What should the RN do first?

Why B is correct

These signs show a tension pneumothorax, a life-threatening emergency that requires prompt decompression.

Why the others are wrong

A. A routine film delays treatment in an unstable patient.

C. More positive pressure worsens the tension and collapses circulation.

D. Suctioning will not relieve trapped air, and delay can be fatal.

Q19Hepatic encephalopathy
A patient with cirrhosis is receiving lactulose for hepatic encephalopathy. Which finding indicates the medication is effective?

Why C is correct

Lactulose traps ammonia in the gut and removes it through stool, which improves mental status.

Why the others are wrong

A. A rising ammonia level shows worsening encephalopathy.

B. That amount signals overdose, with dehydration and electrolyte loss.

D. Absence of stool means ammonia is not being cleared.

Q20Arterial ulcer care
A patient has a painful, pale ulcer with a punched-out appearance on the toes, cool skin, and absent pedal pulses. Which care is most appropriate?

Why C is correct

Arterial ulcers result from poor perfusion, so dependent positioning helps flow and compression would worsen ischemia.

Why the others are wrong

A. Elevation and compression further reduce arterial flow.

B. Debridement of an ischemic wound can enlarge it without revascularization.

D. Heat raises tissue demand in poorly perfused skin and can burn it.

Q21Airborne precautions
A patient is admitted with suspected active pulmonary tuberculosis. Which precautions are appropriate?

Why B is correct

Tuberculosis spreads by small airborne particles, which require an airborne infection isolation room and respiratory protection.

Why the others are wrong

A. Positive pressure pushes infectious air out into the unit.

C. Contact precautions do not address airborne spread.

D. A surgical mask does not filter airborne droplet nuclei.

Q22Insulin mixing
An RN is drawing up regular insulin and NPH insulin in one syringe. Which sequence is correct?

Why B is correct

Drawing the clear, short-acting insulin first prevents contamination of the regular vial with NPH.

Why the others are wrong

A. That order risks contaminating the regular insulin vial with the intermediate-acting insulin.

C. Vigorous shaking causes bubbles and inaccurate dosing, and the vial should be rolled gently.

D. The two can be mixed in one syringe when compatible, which spares the patient an extra injection.

Q23Opioid respiratory depression
A postoperative patient who received IV morphine is difficult to arouse, with a respiratory rate of 6/min. What should the RN do first?

Why C is correct

A respiratory rate of 6 after an opioid indicates respiratory depression requiring airway support and the reversal agent.

Why the others are wrong

A. More opioid would deepen the respiratory depression.

B. An hour of unaddressed hypoventilation can lead to arrest.

D. Flumazenil reverses benzodiazepines, not opioids.

Q24Nasogastric tube placement
Before giving medications through a newly placed nasogastric tube, which method most reliably confirms placement?

Why A is correct

An X-ray is the gold standard because bedside tests can give false reassurance.

Why the others are wrong

B. Speaking does not confirm gastric position and does not rule out airway placement.

C. A tube can enter the lung without causing cough, particularly in sedated patients.

D. The auscultation method is unreliable and can miss lung placement.

Q25Urinary retention
A patient who had spinal surgery has not voided in eight hours and feels suprapubic pressure. Which action comes first?

Why B is correct

A noninvasive scan quantifies urine volume and guides whether catheterization is needed.

Why the others are wrong

A. Manual pressure can injure the bladder and is not an accepted method.

C. A diuretic increases production without relieving the obstruction or retention.

D. More fluid adds to a bladder that is already full.

Q26Femoral access site bleeding
After a cardiac catheterization, blood is spreading under the dressing at the femoral site. What should the RN do first?

Why A is correct

Direct pressure over the vessel stops arterial bleeding and prevents hematoma or hemorrhage.

Why the others are wrong

B. Moving the leg increases bleeding from the access site.

C. Elevation alone does not control active bleeding.

D. Cleaning wastes time while the bleeding continues.

Q27Patient-controlled analgesia
A patient with a patient-controlled analgesia pump is sleepy, and a visiting family member offers to press the button so the patient can rest. What should the RN say?

Why B is correct

A patient who becomes too drowsy stops dosing; a proxy bypasses that built-in safeguard and risks overdose.

Why the others are wrong

A. Changing pump settings requires an order and does not make the method safer.

C. Family-activated dosing removes the safeguard against oversedation.

D. Scheduled pressing ignores the patient's actual pain and sedation level.

Q28Acute kidney injury
A patient with acute kidney injury has a serum potassium of 6.4 mEq/L and peaked T waves on the monitor. Which action is the priority?

Why D is correct

Peaked T waves show cardiac effects of hyperkalemia, which can progress to lethal arrhythmias without prompt therapy.

Why the others are wrong

A. Potassium-sparing diuretics raise potassium further.

B. Additional potassium would worsen the hyperkalemia.

C. Waiting a day risks cardiac arrest.

Q29Fluid volume deficit
An older adult has had diarrhea for three days and now has dry mucous membranes, poor skin turgor, and a heart rate of 112/min. Which finding best confirms dehydration?

Why B is correct

Positional hypotension and concentrated urine reflect reduced circulating volume.

Why the others are wrong

A. Dilute urine suggests excess fluid or a concentrating defect rather than dehydration.

C. Gaining weight points to retention, while dehydration lowers weight.

D. Those findings indicate fluid overload, not deficit.

Q30Hand hygiene with alcohol
An RN has just examined a patient with no known infectious disease and no visible soiling on the hands. Which hand hygiene is appropriate before the next patient?

Why C is correct

Alcohol-based rub is the preferred method when hands are not visibly soiled and spores are not a concern.

Why the others are wrong

A. Water alone does not remove or kill microorganisms.

B. Wiping only moves microorganisms around.

D. Five seconds is far too short for effective washing.

Q31Central line dressing
A patient's central venous catheter dressing is damp and partially lifting at the edge. What should the RN do?

Why C is correct

A compromised dressing allows organisms to track along the catheter, so it must be changed promptly.

Why the others are wrong

A. Taping over a damp, loose dressing leaves the site exposed.

B. A cloth is not sterile and can introduce organisms.

D. Dressings that are damp, soiled, or loose should be changed immediately, regardless of schedule.

Q32Tracheostomy dislodgement
A patient whose tracheostomy was placed three weeks ago coughs forcefully and the tube comes out. The patient is anxious and has noisy breathing. What should the RN do first?

Why B is correct

A mature tract allows reinsertion, and an immediate airway is the priority while help arrives.

Why the others are wrong

A. Oxygen by mouth and nose may not pass the narrowed upper airway and delays reinsertion.

C. Covering the stoma without establishing an airway leaves the patient in distress.

D. Deep suction without an airway can cause trauma and does not restore ventilation.
