Q1
At an outpatient draw station, the requisition is for Taylor Morgan, DOB 08/12/1994. The patient states the same identifiers, but the printed label set reads Taylor Morgen. What should you do before collecting?
Correct answer: C. Step 1: The label has a patient-identifier error before collection begins. Step 2: Patient identity and specimen labeling must match the verified patient exactly. Step 3: The safest action is to correct the label first, then re-verify and collect. a) “Fix it later” is the classic labeling trap; once the specimen is collected, you have created preventable identification risk. b) Covering one label with another creates ambiguity and can cause scanning or identification errors. d) Hand-correcting a printed identifier is not the best answer when the correct action is to generate an accurate label and verify again.
Q2
You leave the room of a patient on suspected C. difficile precautions and remove your gloves. What hand hygiene is required next?
Correct answer: B. Step 1: C. difficile is spore-forming. Step 2: Alcohol products are less reliable for removing spores than soap and water. Step 3: After glove removal, soap-and-water handwashing is the safest answer. a) This is the trap: “not visibly soiled” does not make alcohol hand rub the best choice for C. difficile. c) Gloves reduce contamination but do not replace hand hygiene. d) Disinfectant wipes are not the standard hand-hygiene method for this situation.
Q3
In the middle of a blood draw, you notice that the sharps container opening is jammed and the container appears overfilled. What is the safest immediate plan?
Correct answer: A. Step 1: An overfilled sharps container is a safety hazard. Step 2: OSHA requires sharps containers to be close to the point of use and not allowed to overfill. Step 3: The safe immediate choice is to use another approved container, then report or replace the overfilled one. b) Pushing contents down increases exposure risk. c) Carrying a used needle to another room increases exposure risk during transport and violates safe sharps handling practices. d) Recapping a contaminated needle is unsafe and increases the risk of needlestick injury.
Q4
A patient says, “I fainted the last time,” but insists on standing during venipuncture. What is the best response?
Correct answer: D. Step 1: A history of fainting makes patient positioning a pre-collection safety issue. Step 2: The exam expects you to prevent injury, not just finish the draw. Step 3: Seated or reclined positioning is the safest choice before starting. a) Looking away may reduce anxiety, but standing is still unsafe for a syncope-prone patient. b) Tourniquet changes do not solve the fall risk. c) Keeping a chair nearby does not eliminate fall risk; proper positioning must be established before the procedure begins.
Q5
During venipuncture, the patient reports a sharp, electric pain that radiates into the hand. What should you do first?
Correct answer: A. Step 1: Sharp electric pain radiating into the hand suggests possible nerve contact or injury. Step 2: Continued probing or finishing the tube increases the chance of harm. Step 3: Stop immediately and remove the needle. b) Finishing the tube prioritizes specimen completion over patient safety. c) Attempting to redirect the needle when nerve involvement is suspected can worsen injury; the correct action is immediate removal. d) Hand movement does not correct a suspected nerve contact problem.
Q6
You are asked to collect a legal blood alcohol specimen. Another staff member already cleaned the site with an alcohol pad and the area has dried. What should you do?
Correct answer: C. Step 1: Legal blood alcohol collection has special site-prep rules. Step 2: The key issue is to avoid alcohol-based antisepsis in the collection protocol used for evidentiary testing. Step 3: Re-prep the site with an approved non-alcohol antiseptic per protocol before drawing. a) Even if alcohol has dried, using it violates legal collection protocol and may compromise the validity of the specimen. b) Documentation does not fix a collection-method problem. d) Skipping antisepsis would create an aseptic and evidentiary problem.
Q7
In the lobby, two patients stand up when you call Alex Kim. What should you do next?
Correct answer: B. Step 1: Same-name patients create a high-risk wrong-patient scenario. Step 2: You need person-specific identifiers, such as full name and DOB, matched to the order. Step 3: Positive identification must happen before collection. a) Arrival order has nothing to do with identity verification. c) “Here for blood work” is not a unique identifier. d) Visual recognition is unreliable and not the standard.
Q8
You drew two tubes, walked to the counter to label them, and then realized you were no longer at the bedside. The patient has already left. What should you do?
Correct answer: D. Step 1: The tubes were not labeled in the patient’s presence at the point of collection. Step 2: That creates a specimen identification problem. Step 3: The correct next step is to follow policy for unlabeled or mislabeled specimens, which often means rejection or recollection. a) Memory is not an acceptable patient-identification system. b) Verbal confirmation after the fact does not validate specimen identity because labeling must occur in the patient’s presence at the time of collection. c) Registration staff cannot confirm specimen identity because they did not witness the collection process.
Q9
The patient withdraws consent after you apply the tourniquet but before needle insertion. What is the correct action?
Correct answer: C. Step 1: Consent can be withdrawn before the procedure starts. Step 2: Once the patient refuses, the phlebotomist must stop. Step 3: Remove the tourniquet and follow the refusal process. a) Preparation does not override refusal. b) Collecting “just one tube” is still proceeding without consent. d) Persuasion while you continue ignores the patient’s decision and is not appropriate exam logic.
Q10
You are collecting from a patient in airborne isolation for suspected tuberculosis. What is required at minimum?
Correct answer: B. Step 1: Suspected TB requires airborne precautions. Step 2: Airborne precautions call for a fit-tested N95 or higher-level respirator for personnel. Step 3: Use the respirator and any other facility-required isolation PPE. a) A surgical mask protects against droplets but does not provide the respiratory protection required for airborne pathogens like TB. c) A face shield alone does not provide airborne respiratory protection. d) Gloves alone are inadequate.
Q11
An outpatient requisition is missing the ordering provider information. The patient is seated and ready. What should you do?
Correct answer: A. Step 1: The requisition is incomplete. Step 2: The NHA outline specifically expects order/requisition review and verification before collection. Step 3: Resolve the missing ordering information first. b) “Collect now, fix later” is a common but unsafe processing shortcut. c) Holding a specimen without complete order information can compromise processing and is not appropriate without proper order verification. d) The patient is not the authoritative source for provider-of-record selection.
Q12
A patient has an IV infusing in the right forearm. The left arm is unrestricted and has a good vein. You need a CBC and a CMP. What is the best site choice?
Correct answer: D. Step 1: Blood drawn from an extremity near an active IV can be diluted or contaminated. Step 2: If the opposite arm is available, that is the preferred site. Step 3: Use the left arm. a) Above an IV is not the best answer when a clean opposite arm is available. b) Below an active IV is also at risk for contamination or dilution. c) Switching to the right hand does not correct the IV-related problem if it is still the same extremity.
Q13
Using a butterfly set with tubing, you must collect a PT/INR and the light blue tube is the first required tube. What should you do to help ensure proper fill?
Correct answer: B. Step 1: With a butterfly set, tubing air can affect the first-citrate fill. Step 2: A discard tube is used when protocol requires clearing tubing dead space before the light blue tube. Step 3: That helps preserve the proper blood-to-additive ratio. a) Direct collection may short-fill the citrate tube. c) Drawing lavender first would violate order-of-draw principles. d) Moving the citrate tube later in the sequence increases the risk of additive carryover and breaks the expected routine workflow.
Q14
A light blue tube for coagulation testing is collected but is clearly underfilled. What is the best action?
Correct answer: C. Step 1: Sodium citrate tubes depend on a precise blood-to-additive ratio. Step 2: A clearly underfilled tube may produce invalid coagulation results. Step 3: The best answer is recollection. a) The lab does not simply “math-correct” routine underfill problems. b) Topping off from another tube breaks specimen integrity. d) Spinning early does not repair a ratio error.
Q15
During collection for potassium and lactate, the patient keeps pumping the fist. What should you do?
Correct answer: A. Step 1: Fist pumping can alter analytes, especially lactate and potassium. Step 2: The patient should relax the hand and avoid repeated clenching. Step 3: That reduces preanalytical distortion. b) Encouraging pumping reinforces the error. c) Tightening the tourniquet increases stasis rather than fixing the cause. d) Even minimal repeated movement can alter analytes; the safest instruction is to keep the hand relaxed and still.
Q16
A provider orders PT/INR, CMP, and CBC using standard venipuncture. Which order of draw is correct?
Correct answer: D. Step 1: This is an order-of-draw question: coagulation before serum before EDTA. Step 2: PT/INR uses light blue citrate, CMP is commonly serum/SST, and CBC uses lavender EDTA. Step 3: The correct sequence is light blue, then SST, then lavender. a) Serum before citrate risks improper sequence. b) EDTA before citrate is especially problematic because of additive carryover risk. c) Green is not the requested tube for PT/INR or the best answer for this set.
Q17
Blood flow stops right after you push a new tube onto the multisample needle. The needle is still in the vein. What should you try first?
Correct answer: B. Step 1: Flow stopping right after a new tube is attached means the problem may be the tube, not the vein. Step 2: The first troubleshooting move is to confirm the tube is fully seated and, if necessary, try another tube. Step 3: Only after that would you consider other causes. a) Probing is not the first or safest correction. c) Extra tourniquet time is not the best first troubleshooting step. d) Adjusting needle position prematurely may cause vessel damage; troubleshooting should start with equipment checks first.
Q18
You see a hematoma forming during the draw. What should you do first?
Correct answer: A. Step 1: A developing hematoma means blood is leaking into tissue. Step 2: Continuing the draw worsens the complication. Step 3: Release the tourniquet, remove the needle, and apply firm pressure. b) Completing the tube continues blood leakage into tissue and worsens the hematoma. c) Heat is not the immediate first response during active leakage. d) Bending the elbow does not provide the best direct pressure and may worsen bruising.
Q19
While collecting blood cultures, you cleanse the site and then palpate the vein again without maintaining asepsis. What should you do?
Correct answer: C. Step 1: Blood cultures require strict asepsis. Step 2: Touching the site after preparation compromises the skin antisepsis. Step 3: The safest correction is to re-prep the site and allow proper drying/contact time again. a) Clean gloves do not preserve a contaminated prep site. b) A quick alcohol wipe is not the same as repeating the approved skin-prep procedure. d) Moving slightly away does not solve contamination of the prepared field.
Q20
You are collecting blood cultures with a butterfly set that contains air in the tubing. Which bottle should be filled first?
Correct answer: B. Step 1: With a butterfly set, there is air in the tubing. Step 2: Filling the aerobic bottle first prevents that air from compromising the anaerobic bottle. Step 3: Therefore, aerobic first is the best answer. a) Filling the anaerobic bottle first allows air in the tubing to enter the bottle, which can compromise the specimen. c) Alternating small amounts is not standard bottle collection technique. d) Order does matter in this setup.
Q21
A timed trough drug level is ordered. The patient says the medication was taken 30 minutes ago. What should you do?
Correct answer: D. Step 1: A trough level is meant to represent the drug concentration just before the next dose. Step 2: If the medication was taken 30 minutes ago, the timing is inappropriate for a trough. Step 3: The phlebotomist should follow procedure and coordinate the correct timing, not relabel the specimen as something it is not. a) Calling it a trough would be inaccurate. b) Documenting the incorrect timing does not correct the fact that the specimen does not represent a true trough level. c) Instructing dose changes is outside phlebotomy scope.
Q22
A patient arrives for a 2-hour postprandial glucose draw and says they finished eating 3 hours ago. What should you do?
Correct answer: C. Step 1: A 2-hour postprandial specimen depends on timing from the meal. Step 2: At 3 hours after eating, the collection no longer matches the ordered timing. Step 3: The correct response is to follow procedure for a mistimed specimen, which may mean clarifying or rescheduling. a) “Late” documentation does not make it a valid 2-hour specimen. b) Restarting timing on your own changes the collection plan without authorization. d) Converting it to a fasting test is outside scope.
Q23
You need an adult capillary puncture, but the patient’s hands are cold and perfusion is poor. What is the best preparation?
Correct answer: A. Step 1: Cold fingers often produce poor capillary flow. Step 2: Warming the site or otherwise improving circulation helps produce a usable specimen. Step 3: That is the safest and most specimen-friendly first step. b) Performing a second puncture without first improving circulation may result in another inadequate specimen. c) The thumb is generally avoided for routine capillary puncture. d) Aggressive massage can hemolyze or dilute the specimen.
Q24
During a fingerstick, what should you do with the first drop of blood?
Correct answer: B. Step 1: The first drop may contain tissue fluid or debris. Step 2: Wiping it away improves specimen quality. Step 3: Then collect subsequent free-flowing drops. a) Using the first drop risks contamination. c) Recleaning after puncture is unnecessary and can disrupt collection. d) Even gentle pressure can introduce tissue fluid contamination if excessive; the preferred method is free-flowing blood.
Q25
After venipuncture, a patient who takes an anticoagulant continues to ooze from the site. What is the best action?
Correct answer: D. Step 1: Continued oozing after venipuncture is a post-procedure safety issue, especially in anticoagulated patients. Step 2: The immediate priority is hemostasis. Step 3: Maintain firm pressure longer, then reassess before discharge. a) A tourniquet is not a pressure dressing. b) Bending the elbow is not the best way to control bleeding and may worsen bruising. c) Allowing the patient to leave with active bleeding risks continued blood loss and complications.
Q26
A bilirubin specimen must be transported through a brightly lit area. What should you do?
Correct answer: A. Step 1: Bilirubin is a light-sensitive analyte. Step 2: Exposure to bright light can degrade the specimen. Step 3: Protecting it from light preserves specimen integrity. b) Speed does not prevent light degradation; bilirubin must be protected from light regardless of transport time. c) Ice may be required for some tests, but light protection is the key issue here. d) Warming is unrelated and may be inappropriate.
Q27
An SST is centrifuged within minutes of collection before it has fully clotted. What problem is most likely?
Correct answer: C. Step 1: Serum must clot adequately before centrifugation. Step 2: If it is spun too soon, fibrin strands or microclots may remain. Step 3: Those can interfere with testing or instrument performance. a) EDTA contamination is a different preanalytical problem. b) Citrate carryover is unrelated to premature serum centrifugation. d) Separator gel does not reliably “fix” incomplete clotting.
Q28
Ammonia is ordered. Which handling step is most appropriate after collection?
Correct answer: B. Step 1: Ammonia is highly sensitive to handling conditions. Step 2: Public handling instructions call for immediate icing and prompt processing. Step 3: Delays or warm handling can produce falsely increased results. a) Even prompt transport at room temperature can lead to falsely elevated ammonia levels due to ongoing metabolism. c) Light protection is not the key handling issue here. d) Ammonia is not collected as a serum-clot specimen in this workflow.
Q29
In a chain-of-custody urine drug screen, the specimen temperature is outside the acceptable range when checked within 4 minutes. What should you do?
Correct answer: D. Step 1: Chain-of-custody urine collection has strict temperature rules. Step 2: Temperature must be checked within 4 minutes, and out-of-range temperature triggers the required protocol response. Step 3: The collector must follow the chain-of-custody procedure, including the immediate next collection step required by rule. a) Complete documentation does not override temperature validity requirements in chain-of-custody procedures. b) Rechecking later defeats the purpose of the immediate validity check. c) Discarding without proper documentation breaks chain-of-custody integrity.
Q30
A patient asks you to interpret their A1c and tell them whether they have diabetes. What is the best response?
Correct answer: C. Step 1: The question asks for result interpretation, not specimen collection procedure. Step 2: The official NHA outline places the phlebotomist role in identification, collection, safety, and processing. Step 3: The in-scope response is to refer interpretation to the ordering provider. a) Giving a diagnostic opinion exceeds phlebotomy scope. b) Explaining the test without interpretation can still lead the patient to draw conclusions, which is outside the phlebotomist’s scope. d) That response is unprofessional and not patient-centered.
