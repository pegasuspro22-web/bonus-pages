Domain 2 of 5 · 25 questions

Intake, Assessment, and Diagnosis

Q16Psychotic symptoms and substances
Roommates bring in a 24-year-old man who hears voices and believes the neighbors are watching him. The symptoms began two days after he started using methamphetamine heavily and have eased over four days of abstinence. Which diagnosis is most strongly supported?

Why D is correct

Psychotic symptoms that began during heavy stimulant use and fade with abstinence point to a substance-induced cause.

Why the others are wrong

A. This diagnosis applies only when the symptoms are not attributable to a substance.

B. Delusional disorder involves persistent delusions without prominent hallucinations, lasting at least a month.

C. Schizophrenia needs six months of disturbance and symptoms not better explained by a substance.

Q17Suicide risk and level of care
A 34-year-old woman with moderate depressive symptoms says she sometimes wishes she would not wake up. She has no plan, no intent, no prior attempts, good family support, and agrees to work on a safety plan. What is the most appropriate level of care?

Why C is correct

Passive ideation without plan or intent and with strong protective factors fits the least restrictive effective level of care.

Why the others are wrong

A. Monthly contact is too infrequent for a client with current suicidal thoughts.

B. Hospitalization is not the least restrictive option when risk is low and protective factors are strong.

D. Risk can change, so ongoing reassessment is part of care even when ideation is passive.

Q18Trauma and dissociation
A woman attending an intensive outpatient program after an assault reports nightmares and moments when she “zones out” during conversations. What is the best next step?

Why A is correct

Assessment of trauma, dissociation, functioning, and safety guides treatment without disrupting care that is working.

Why the others are wrong

B. Trauma symptoms are often central to the presentation and should be assessed, not excluded.

C. Intensive trauma processing before assessing dissociation and safety can destabilize the client.

D. Dissociation alone does not require hospitalization when safety and functioning are adequate.

Q19Co-occurring disorders
A 20-year-old college student presents with low mood and anxiety and reports daily cannabis use. Which information is most important to gather next?

Why C is correct

The timing of symptoms relative to substance use separates an independent disorder from a substance-related one.

Why the others are wrong

A. Grades show impact but do not establish whether symptoms are independent of use.

B. Family opinions do not bear on diagnosis or treatment planning.

D. Treatment history helps but cannot determine the diagnosis without the use and symptom timeline.

Q20Partner violence screening
A woman asks for couple counseling, saying her husband is “very controlling” and checks her phone each day. Which information is most important before the counselor agrees to joint sessions?

Why A is correct

Safety screening for violence, done privately, determines whether couple counseling is appropriate at all.

Why the others are wrong

B. Disagreement about money is secondary to the question of safety.

C. These details do not address safety or the suitability of joint sessions.

D. Raising possible abuse in a joint session can put the woman at risk.

Q21Chronic suicidal thoughts
A client with borderline personality traits has chronic urges to self-harm. She has no current plan or intent, lives in stable housing, and engages well in weekly therapy. What is the most appropriate level of care?

Why C is correct

Chronic ideation without acute risk is managed in outpatient care with monitoring and a shared safety plan.

Why the others are wrong

A. Admission is reserved for acute risk and would be more restrictive than necessary.

B. Risk fluctuates, so monitoring continues even when ideation is long-standing.

D. Outpatient work is an accepted setting for these clients, and she is engaged.

Q22Assessment instruments
At intake, a client scores 28 on the Yale-Brown Obsessive Compulsive Scale (Y-BOCS). How should the counselor interpret the result?

Why C is correct

A score in this range reflects severe symptoms and serves as a starting point for monitoring response.

Why the others are wrong

A. A symptom scale cannot rule out other conditions.

B. The Y-BOCS measures obsessive-compulsive symptom severity, not personality.

D. An instrument supports but never replaces a diagnostic interview.

Q23Family assessment tools
A family therapist wants to map patterns of substance use, divorce, and estrangement across three generations of one family. Which tool is most appropriate?

Why A is correct

A genogram maps relationships and recurring patterns across generations.

Why the others are wrong

B. The mental status examination records a client's current functioning, not family patterns.

C. This scale screens suicide risk and does not map family history.

D. It screens an individual's drinking and does not show multigenerational patterns.

Q24Dissociation screening
A client reports losing blocks of time and finding items at home that she does not remember buying. Which screening instrument is most appropriate?

Why C is correct

This instrument is designed to screen for dissociative experiences such as memory gaps.

Why the others are wrong

A. The PHQ-9 screens depressive symptoms.

B. The AUDIT screens hazardous drinking.

D. The GAD-7 screens anxiety severity, not memory loss or dissociation.

Q25Differential diagnosis
A 45-year-old woman was laid off two months ago. She is anxious and tearful, has trouble working, and has no suicidal thoughts. Her speech is normal and her thinking is organized. She does not meet criteria for major depression. Which diagnosis fits best?

Why D is correct

Emotional symptoms within three months of an identifiable stressor, not meeting another disorder's criteria, indicate an adjustment disorder.

Why the others are wrong

A. The full symptom criteria for major depression are not met.

B. A layoff is a stressor but not the traumatic exposure this diagnosis requires.

C. This requires excessive worry for at least six months, and her symptoms are two months old.

Q26Medical contributors to symptoms
A 38-year-old woman has new-onset anxiety, a fine tremor, unintended weight loss, and trouble tolerating heat. What is the counselor's best next step?

Why C is correct

These features suggest a medical cause that must be ruled out before a psychiatric diagnosis is made.

Why the others are wrong

A. A medical contributor has not been excluded, so the diagnosis is premature.

B. Personality testing does not explain physical findings.

D. Delay is inappropriate when physical signs suggest a medical condition.

Q27Mental status and risk
A 16-year-old with a flat affect says he sometimes thinks his family would be better off without him. After a direct assessment he reports no plan, no intent, and no attempts, and he agrees to a safety plan with his parents. What is the best course?

Why C is correct

Low-risk ideation with protective factors and an agreed safety plan is managed in outpatient care with follow-up.

Why the others are wrong

A. Asking directly about suicide does not increase risk and is required.

B. Hospitalization is not the least restrictive option for low-risk ideation.

D. Wide disclosure breaches confidentiality without a clinical need.

Q28Biopsychosocial interview
A 62-year-old man comes to counseling two months after a heart attack and says he has felt “off” since. Which question best fits a biopsychosocial interview?

Why D is correct

This open question covers the biological, psychological, and social areas of the client's life.

Why the others are wrong

A. A “why” question that blames the client closes down exploration.

B. Starting with childhood ignores the presenting concern.

C. This closed question covers a narrow medical detail.

Q29Opening the clinical interview
A 19-year-old college student says she cannot stop worrying since she moved out of her parents' home. Which question best explores how the problem began?

Why A is correct

This open question explores onset, context, and impact on functioning.

Why the others are wrong

B. This suggests a solution before the problem is understood.

C. This closed question does not explore onset or impact.

D. Comparison with others is judgmental and blocks disclosure.

Q30Obsessions and compulsions
A client says she repeatedly checks that the stove is off before she can leave home. Which question best assesses whether this is a compulsion?

Why B is correct

Time consumed and anxiety relief are core features that separate compulsions from habits.

Why the others are wrong

A. This challenges the client instead of assessing symptom features.

C. Telling someone to stop shows misunderstanding of compulsions.

D. Family patterns matter less than the symptom's time and distress.

Q31Cultural formulation and risk
A recently arrived immigrant from Guatemala says her “nerves are broken” and she wishes she could disappear. What is the best way to proceed?

Why C is correct

Cultural understanding and direct risk assessment are both needed, and neither replaces the other.

Why the others are wrong

A. Background alone is not a basis for hospitalization.

B. Dismissing her explanation damages trust and cultural validity.

D. A statement that could signal suicide must always be assessed directly.

Q32Eating disorder diagnosis
A 16-year-old girl has a body mass index of 15.8, an intense fear of gaining weight, and restricts her eating. She reports no binge eating or purging in the past three months. Which diagnosis is most strongly supported?

Why D is correct

Low weight, fear of weight gain, and restriction without binge or purge behavior fit the restricting type.

Why the others are wrong

A. Bulimia is not diagnosed when the person is significantly underweight.

B. This type requires recurrent binge eating or purging, which is absent.

C. This lacks the fear of weight gain that is present here.

Q33Observation in assessment
A couple says, “We fight about everything.” After taking histories, which additional information would be most useful?

Why D is correct

Direct observation of the interaction shows the pattern that self-report may hide.

Why the others are wrong

A. Family history can matter but does not reveal the present pattern.

B. A count says little about how the conflict actually unfolds.

C. Assigning blame does not reveal the interaction pattern.

Q34Interpreting mental status findings
A widow says she briefly heard her husband call her name the evening after the funeral and knew it was not real. She is oriented, her thinking is organized, and her affect is sad. What do these findings suggest?

Why C is correct

A brief perception during bereavement with preserved insight is within the range of normal grief.

Why the others are wrong

A. She is oriented and knew the experience was not real.

B. Nothing in the history suggests substance use.

D. Intact reality testing and organized thinking argue against psychosis.

Q35Sleep complaints
A 40-year-old shift worker reports trouble falling asleep for three weeks since a conflict with his supervisor. How should the counselor interpret the problem?

Why D is correct

A three-week complaint needs a fuller assessment, and a diagnosis cannot be made until criteria and contributors are checked.

Why the others are wrong

A. Sleep apnea requires medical evaluation and cannot be confirmed from history alone.

B. Significant sleep disruption deserves assessment of causes.

C. The diagnosis requires symptoms lasting at least three months.

Q36Interpreting a screening score
At intake, a client scores 16 on the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9). How should this be interpreted?

Why A is correct

A score in this range reflects clinically significant depressive symptoms that need a full evaluation.

Why the others are wrong

B. A screening score does not replace a diagnostic interview.

C. A score of 16 is well above the range of normal variation.

D. The PHQ-9 measures depressive symptoms.

Q37Monitoring risk during treatment
After three months of improvement, a client loses his job and his suicidal thoughts increase on the monthly outcome measure. What is the best action?

Why B is correct

A change in stressors and scores calls for direct reassessment and added support.

Why the others are wrong

A. Earlier gains do not outweigh new risk.

C. Admission depends on the assessed level of risk, not a score change alone.

D. Waiting leaves a possible rise in risk unaddressed.

Q38Alcohol use assessment
A woman says she has “a few drinks to unwind” each evening, and her partner is concerned. What is the best next step?

Why B is correct

A validated screen plus a collaborative conversation gives objective data without labeling her.

Why the others are wrong

A. A diagnosis cannot be made without assessment.

C. Secret monitoring harms trust and is unethical.

D. A partner's concern and daily drinking call for further assessment.

Q39Level of care and withdrawal
A man who drinks heavily every day, with his last drink 14 hours ago, shows tremors and sweating. He and his wife ask to begin couple counseling today. What is the best action?

Why D is correct

Alcohol withdrawal can be medically dangerous, so medical evaluation comes first.

Why the others are wrong

A. Medical risk takes priority over relationship work.

B. Abrupt cessation of heavy drinking can cause dangerous withdrawal.

C. Withdrawal symptoms require medical assessment, not relaxation skills.

Q40Outcome measures
In the first session, a client scores 14 on the GAD-7 and asks what the number means. What is the best response?

Why A is correct

Explaining the score as a baseline for tracking change is accurate and collaborative.

Why the others are wrong

B. Outcome scores monitor the individual's change, not ranking.

C. A score is not a diagnosis and is expected to change with treatment.

D. A single score cannot predict treatment length.
