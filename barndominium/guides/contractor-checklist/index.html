Contractor Communication Checklist

How builders work, what a good bid and contract contain, and how to communicate so that the house you get is the house you meant.

The relationship with your builder will shape your project more than any material you choose. Most disputes between owners and contractors are not about bad faith; they come from two reasonable people who understood the same job differently and discovered it too late. This guide explains how a builder's business works, how to read bids and contracts, and how to communicate during construction so that misunderstandings are caught while they are still cheap. The checklist in Part 2 follows the same order and can be printed for every meeting.

Part 1The guide

How a builder sees your project

Understanding the other side of the table makes every conversation easier. A general contractor rarely builds a house with only their own crew. They coordinate a sequence of specialists, such as excavators, concrete crews, steel erectors, insulation installers, plumbers, electricians and HVAC contractors, each with their own schedule and other jobs. The builder's real product is coordination: the right people, materials and inspections arriving in the right order.

A builder's margin pays for that coordination, their insurance, their office and their risk. When an owner pushes the price below a sustainable level, the margin does not disappear; it moves into cheaper materials, rushed work or change orders. A fair price with a well-organized builder is almost always cheaper in the end than a low price with a struggling one.

Builders also juggle several projects. Your job competes for attention with others, and the owners who get the best service are usually the ones who make decisions on time, communicate clearly and pay as agreed. You are not only choosing a builder; you are becoming a client that a good builder wants to serve well.

Choosing the builder

Start by checking the basics: a license for residential work where you are building, liability insurance and workers' compensation coverage, and a track record with barndominiums or metal buildings. Metal shells, spray foam and slab plumbing have their own pitfalls, and experience with them matters.

The most valuable research is a visit to finished projects and a conversation with the owners, ideally without the builder present. Ask how the schedule went, how changes were handled, how quickly problems were fixed and whether they would hire the builder again. Ask what surprised them. People who have just finished a house are usually happy to talk, and their answers tell you more than any brochure.

Then interview the builder. Ask who will be your day-to-day contact, how often you will meet on site, and which parts of the work their own crews perform. One question is especially revealing: what are the three most common problems on your barndominium projects, and how do you prevent them? A builder with real experience answers immediately and specifically. A vague answer is a warning.

Reading a bid

A bid is only comparable with another bid if both describe the same house. That is why the first step is to give every builder the same drawings and the same specifications, and to ask for prices broken down by phase. A single lump sum tells you very little.

Look carefully at what is included and what is excluded. Site work, utility connections, permits, driveways and septic systems are common exclusions, and each one can be a large number. Read the specifications line by line: panel gauge, insulation type and thickness, window series, HVAC size. Two bids for a 1,800 square foot barndominium can differ by tens of thousands of dollars simply because one includes a well and the other assumes you will hire your own driller.

Allowances, explained

An allowance is a placeholder amount for items you have not chosen yet, such as cabinets, flooring, tile or light fixtures. If the bid includes a $12,000 allowance for cabinets and the cabinets you choose cost $18,500, you pay the $6,500 difference, often plus the builder's markup. Low allowances make a bid look cheaper while guaranteeing extra costs later. Ask for allowances that match the finishes you actually want, and compare them across bids.

Two bids, one houseBuilder A quotes $312,000 with a $6,000 lighting allowance and $4 per square foot for flooring. Builder B quotes $329,000 with $11,000 and $7. For an owner with mid-range taste on 1,800 square feet of flooring, Builder A's price climbs by about $10,400 once real selections are made: $5,000 more for lighting and about $5,400 more for flooring. The $17,000 gap shrinks to about $6,600, and only reading the allowances reveals it.

The contract, without the legal jargon

Most residential contracts fall into two families. In a fixed-price contract, the builder agrees to build the defined house for a set amount, and takes on the risk of cost overruns within that definition. In a cost-plus contract, you pay the actual costs plus a fee or percentage, which offers transparency but leaves the risk with you; a cost-plus contract with a guaranteed maximum price is a common middle ground. Neither is better in every case, but you should know which one you are signing and what happens when costs move.

Whatever the type, a few elements protect you. The drawings and specifications should be attached and named as the definition of the work, so there is no argument later about what was promised. Payments should be tied to completed, inspected milestones that match your lender's draws. Lien waivers should be collected with every payment, so that a subcontractor or supplier who was not paid cannot place a lien on your property. Changes should require a written change order signed before the work. The warranty should state its length, what it covers and how to make a claim.

Read the clauses on delays, disputes and termination slowly. They matter only when something goes wrong, which is exactly when you will wish you had understood them. If anything is unclear, an hour with a construction attorney before signing is among the best money you will spend on the whole project.

Communicating during the build

Good communication on a job site is less about personality than about routine. Agree on one main channel, usually email or a shared project app, and one point of contact on each side. Text messages and conversations on site are fine for quick questions, but anything that affects price, schedule or quality should end up written in the main channel.

A short weekly meeting, ideally on site, prevents most problems. Use the same agenda every time: what was finished, what starts next, which decisions are due from you, any changes, the next payment and the next inspection. Then send a brief follow-up email the same day summarizing what was decided. It takes ten minutes, and it turns every conversation into a record both sides can refer to.

Make decisions on time. A selections schedule from the builder tells you when each choice is needed: windows early, cabinets and tile later, paint near the end. Missing those dates is one of the main causes of delays, and delays cost the builder money that tends to come back to you.

When something looks wrong on site, ask before you assume. Photograph it, describe it calmly in writing and ask the builder how they intend to handle it. Most issues are resolved quickly when they are raised early and respectfully. Save firmness for the rare cases that need it.

Disagreements and closing out

Even good relationships hit disagreements. Keep them about the facts: what the contract and drawings say, what was agreed in writing, what the photos show. Separate the issue from the person. Propose a solution rather than only a complaint. If you cannot agree, follow the dispute process in the contract instead of withholding payment informally, which can put you in breach yourself.

The end of the project needs as much structure as the start. Walk the house room by room with the builder and write a punch list of everything that remains to be finished or corrected, signed by both of you. Confirm the final inspection and the certificate of occupancy. Collect the final lien waivers before the last payment. Ask for every manual, warranty, paint color and model number, and keep them in a house binder. Put warranty end dates on your calendar, and schedule a walk-through shortly before the first-year warranty expires, when any settling, cracks or adjustments can still be fixed at no cost.
