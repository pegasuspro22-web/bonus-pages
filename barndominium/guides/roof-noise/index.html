Roof Noise & Sound Control

Why metal roofs and open rooms get loud, how sound really travels, and where your acoustic budget does the most good.

Ask people why they hesitate about a barndominium and noise is often near the top of the list: rain hammering on steel, voices echoing in a huge great room, the shop compressor heard from the bedroom. All of these are real, and all of them are solved problems. This guide explains how sound behaves in a building, in words a homeowner can use with a builder, so that you know which upgrades matter for your house and which are money spent for little gain. At the end you will find a practical checklist for the design and framing stages.

Part 1The guide

How sound behaves, in five ideas

Acoustics has a reputation for being complicated, but almost every decision in a house comes down to five simple ideas.

Sound is vibration. It travels through air as pressure waves and through solid materials as vibration. A raindrop striking a panel and a voice in the next room are both vibrations looking for a path to your ear.

Mass blocks sound. Heavy, dense layers are hard to set in motion, so less vibration passes through them. Two layers of drywall stop more sound than one; a wood deck under roof panels stops more than steel alone.

Separation blocks sound. When two layers are not rigidly connected, vibration has a harder time crossing from one to the other. Staggered studs, resilient channels and a ceiling hung below an insulated roof all use this principle.

Absorption calms a room. Soft, porous materials such as insulation, fabric and felt turn sound energy into a tiny amount of heat. Inside a wall cavity they stop the cavity from ringing; in a room they shorten echoes.

Where air goes, sound goes. A gap under a door or around a pipe can undo a well-built wall. Airtightness, which the moisture guide asks you to pursue anyway, is also one of the cheapest acoustic upgrades there is.

Key ideaMass, separation, absorption and airtightness. Every quiet-house technique in this guide is one of these four, or a combination of them.

Making sense of the numbers

Two numbers appear when people talk about noise, and a rough feel for them helps you judge claims.

Sound level is measured in decibels. The scale is logarithmic, so the numbers behave in unexpected ways. An increase of about 10 decibels is heard as roughly twice as loud, and a drop of 10 as roughly half. A difference of 3 decibels is barely noticeable. This is why a small upgrade that removes 3 decibels may disappoint, while a change of assembly that removes 10 to 15 transforms a room.

Walls and floors are rated by STC, Sound Transmission Class. The higher the number, the less airborne sound passes through. The book's wall table shows the useful range for a house: the low 30s, where you can follow a conversation next door, up to the 50s, where loud speech is barely audible. Ratings are measured in laboratories; real walls usually perform a few points worse because of outlets, gaps and doors, which is one more reason to seal them.

Rain on a roof is a different kind of noise, called impact noise, and STC does not describe it well. For roofs, think in terms of assemblies, from a bare panel to a deck with a finished ceiling, as the comparison table in Part 2 does.

The roof: from drum to background

A thin steel panel supported only at its purlins behaves like the skin of a drum. Rain and hail set it vibrating, and it radiates that vibration straight down into the room. The cure works on three levels, and each level adds to the one before.

First, damp the panel. Closed-cell foam sprayed on the underside bonds to the steel and stiffens it, so it rings far less. This is the same foam that solves condensation, so in living space you get the acoustic benefit almost for free. Insulated metal panels behave the same way.

Second, add mass. A solid deck of plywood or OSB under the panels, with an underlayment, is the way metal roofs are put on conventional houses and the quietest option. On a steel-frame barndominium it is an extra step and an extra cost, which is why it is usually reserved for the rooms where quiet matters most.

Third, separate the room from the roof. A finished ceiling with insulation above it puts a buffer of air and material between the panels and your ears. This is the single most effective step for most houses, and it is where the dramatic open-to-the-panels look costs you dearly in a storm. You can still show the structure: leave the trusses or beams exposed and finish between them with boards or drywall.

What about hail?

Hail is the extreme case: large, hard impacts. In hail country, a deck under the roof over bedrooms is worth pricing seriously, and the ceiling below should be insulated and finished. Ask your insurer as well; some offer better rates for impact-rated roofing, and the same details help with noise.

The great room: taming echo

An empty barndominium great room is one of the most reverberant spaces a family will ever live in: a concrete floor, tall drywall, big windows and a high ceiling, all hard and reflective. A sound made in the middle of it bounces from surface to surface for a long time, and new sounds pile on top of the old ones before they fade. The result is a room where everyone talks louder, the television goes up, and conversation at a full table becomes tiring.

The cure is not to make the room dead like a recording studio, but to give it enough soft and irregular surfaces that sounds fade quickly. Large rugs, upholstered furniture, heavy curtains and full bookshelves do most of the work. A wood board or slat ceiling with insulation or acoustic felt behind it is especially effective because sound collects high in tall rooms, where furniture never reaches.

Plan for these from the start rather than treating them as decoration. Rug sizes affect furniture layout, a slat ceiling has to be framed for, and curtain tracks need blocking in the walls. Then live in the room for a few weeks before adding acoustic panels. Many rooms are transformed by a rug and a sofa alone, and the clap test described in Part 2 tells you whether more is needed.

Between rooms: layout first, walls second

The open plan that makes a barndominium feel generous also lets sound travel. The best protection costs nothing: arranging the plan so that quiet rooms never share a wall with noisy ones. Bedrooms at the ends of the building, closets and bathrooms as buffers, the mechanical room and laundry away from sleeping areas, and never a bedroom against the shop or garage. A closet full of clothes is an excellent sound barrier.

Construction then finishes the job. While the framing is open, filling interior walls around bedrooms, bathrooms and the office with sound batts is one of the cheapest upgrades in the whole house. A second layer of drywall on one side, or resilient channels, moves those walls into the range where loud speech becomes faint. After the walls are closed, the same improvements cost several times more.

Do not let the details undo the wall. Choose solid-core doors for bedrooms, bathrooms and the office. Offset electrical boxes on shared walls instead of placing them back to back. Seal around pipes and ducts. Keep drain lines out of the walls next to the dining table, or wrap them, because a plastic drain pipe carries every flush into the room.

Machines, the shop and where to spend

In a quiet, well-insulated house you will hear things you never noticed in a drafty one: the air handler starting, the washer spinning, the well pump. Put mechanical equipment away from bedrooms, on isolation pads, and ask the HVAC contractor for a lined return and a flexible connection at the air handler so fan noise does not travel down the ducts. Generous ducts with slow air are quieter than small ducts with fast air.

The wall between an attached shop or garage and the house is required to resist fire, and the same construction stops fumes and noise: insulation, fire-rated drywall, a solid self-closing door with seals all around. Build it as one barrier doing three jobs.

If the budget is tight, spend in this order. First the layout, which is free. Then a finished, insulated ceiling under the roof over living space. Then sound batts in interior walls around bedrooms and baths, and solid-core doors. Then a deck over the primary bedroom. Soft finishes in the great room can come in stages after you move in. Retrofitting a roof or a closed wall is the expensive path, so decide those items before drywall.

Key ideaDecide roof and wall acoustics before the drywall goes up. Everything else can be improved later, one room at a time.

Echo in big rooms

Concrete floors, tall drywall and large windows reflect almost all sound. A great room needs soft and broken-up surfaces to sound comfortable. You do not need studio panels: the everyday materials of a furnished home do most of the work if you plan for enough of them.

Machines and the shop

Two tests you can do yourselfThe clap test: clap once in the empty great room; a long ringing tail means it needs soft surfaces. The hose test: before the ceiling is finished, have someone spray a garden hose on the roof above a bedroom while you listen inside. You will hear what rain will sound like while there is still time to add a deck or more insulation.
