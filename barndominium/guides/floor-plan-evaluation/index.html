Floor Plan Evaluation

How to read any barndominium plan like a professional: zones, flow, light, air, storage and the future, before you pay for drawings.

A floor plan is a promise about how your days will feel. Within its lines are decisions about where the morning sun lands, how far you carry the groceries, whether the television reaches the bedrooms and how much the plumbing costs. Most people judge a plan by how it looks on the screen. This guide teaches you to judge it by how it will work, using the same lenses designers use. Part 2 turns them into a scorecard you can fill in for any plan in a few minutes.

Why plans fool us

Floor plans are drawn from above, a viewpoint nobody ever has in a real house. From up there, a long corridor looks like a neat line, a kitchen island looks generous whatever its clearances, and a great room facing west looks the same as one facing south. The drawing hides time, movement, sound and sunlight, which are exactly the things that make a house pleasant or tiring.

Marketing plans make this worse. They are drawn to sell, often with furniture shown smaller than real furniture, rooms labeled with flattering names and dimensions that may be measured to the center of walls. A plan that works on paper can still put the only path from the garage to the bedrooms right through the kitchen.

The cure is to read a plan actively, asking specific questions in a specific order. Professional designers do this almost unconsciously. The following chapters spell out their questions so you can ask them too.

The first lens: zones

Every house contains three kinds of space. Public space is where the household gathers and guests are welcomed: the great room, kitchen and dining. Private space is where people sleep, bathe and work quietly: bedrooms, primary bath and office. Service space supports the household: mudroom, laundry, pantry, mechanical room and, in a barndominium, often the shop or garage.

A good plan groups each kind together and puts buffers between them. Bedrooms cluster at one end or on one side, separated from the great room by closets, bathrooms or a short hall. Service rooms sit between the outside entries and the living space, so that dirt, noise and fumes stop before they reach it. In the Floor Plan Explorer, the zone layer shades these three areas in different colors, and a well-zoned plan shows them as three clear blocks rather than a patchwork.

Barndominiums add a special case: the shop. Treat it as a service zone with its own rules. It should connect to the house through a mudroom, never directly into the kitchen or a bedroom hall, and the wall between them must resist fire, fumes and sound.

The second lens: flow

Flow is how people move through the house. Trace the trips that happen every day: arriving with groceries, going to bed, getting up at night, letting the dog out, carrying laundry, coming in from the shop with dirty boots. Draw them on a printout of the plan with a pencil. Good flow has short, direct paths that do not cross each other unnecessarily and never pass through a bedroom or bathroom to reach somewhere else.

Watch the kitchen in particular. The work area between sink, range and refrigerator should not be a corridor. If the route from the back door to the bedrooms runs between the island and the range, someone will be standing in the way every evening.

Check clearances as you go. Walkways of at least 36 inches, kitchen aisles of 42 to 48 inches and space to open doors without hitting another door, a toilet or a person are the difference between a plan that works and one that feels cramped despite generous square footage. The size table in Part 2 gives the numbers.

Key ideaDraw the daily trips on the plan with a pencil. Where lines cross, crowd or pass through private rooms, the plan needs work.

The third and fourth lenses: light and air

Sunlight changes the character of a room more than any finish. In most of the United States, the main living spaces are happiest facing roughly south, where a modest roof overhang lets in low winter sun and blocks high summer sun. East light is gentle and welcome in kitchens and breakfast areas. West light is hot and low in the afternoon; large unshaded west glass is a common cause of overheated great rooms. North light is steady and cool, good for offices and studios.

Because a plan rarely shows north, find it before you judge anything. Then imagine the sun moving across the drawing through the day and the seasons. If the house will sit on a specific lot, rotate the plan to match the real site, including views, neighbors and the road.

Air follows light. Rooms with windows on two walls can be cross-ventilated on mild days. Tall rooms benefit from ceiling fans to mix air that would otherwise stratify, with warm air collecting under the ceiling. And every bedroom needs a return path for the air system when its door is closed, through a transfer grille or a dedicated return, or the room will be stuffy and the system noisy.

Storage, cost and the future

Storage is where open barndominium plans most often disappoint. Big rooms with few walls leave little place for things. Look for a coat closet near each entry, a pantry near the kitchen, linen storage near the bathrooms and real closets in bedrooms. A mudroom with lockers or hooks keeps shop and farm clutter out of the living space.

The plan also sets much of the cost. A simple rectangle with a single roofline is the most economical shape for a steel or post-frame building. Grouping the kitchen, bathrooms and laundry along one area of the slab keeps plumbing runs short. Every jog in the outline, extra corner and long separate drain run adds cost without adding space.

Finally, think ten and twenty years ahead. Will children grow up and leave, or parents move in? Could one bedroom become an office? Would someone with a knee injury manage the house? A bedroom and full bathroom on the main floor, a step-free entry, doors of 36 inches and a bathroom with room to turn make a house easier to live in at every age, and they cost very little when planned from the start.

Reading dimensions and using the scorecard

Before you decide anything, make sure you know what the numbers mean. Ask whether room dimensions are measured to the finished wall surface, to the studs or to the wall centers; the difference can be several inches in each direction. Never scale a printed drawing with a ruler, because printing changes the scale. Trust written dimensions, and when one is missing, ask for it.

Compare room sizes with real furniture, not with the pieces drawn on the plan. Measure the bed, sofa and table you own or intend to buy, and check that they fit with comfortable clearance. A secondary bedroom that is legal at 10 by 10 feet is tight with a full bed and a desk.

Then use the scorecard in Part 2. It asks twelve questions across zones, flow, light, air, comfort, storage and cost, each scored from 0 to 2. Score every plan you consider, including the ones in the book. A plan scoring 20 or more out of 24 is ready for detailed drawings once any zeros are fixed. Between 14 and 19 it needs changes, and the list of ones and zeros becomes your brief for the designer. Below 14, the layout has basic problems and comparing it with another plan is the wiser move.

Key ideaScore every plan the same way, write down every zero, and take that list to your designer. It is the fastest way to turn a nice drawing into a house that works.

Walk the plan first

Put your finger on the front door and move through an ordinary day: bring in groceries, go to bed, get up at night for water, let the dog out, carry the laundry, come in from the shop with dirty boots. Note every time you pass through a room you have no business in, squeeze past furniture or open a door into something. Then score.
