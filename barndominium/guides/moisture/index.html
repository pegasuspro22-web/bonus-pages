Moisture & Condensation Prevention

How water moves through a metal building, why condensation starts, and how to stop it before it costs you a ceiling.

Most barndominium owners who fight water inside the house never had a leak. The roof is sound, the windows are tight, and still there are drips on cold mornings, stains on the ceiling and a musty corner in a closet. The water came from the air. This guide explains, in plain language, how moisture behaves in a metal building, how to tell which problem you have, and which decisions protect you for the life of the house. The book gives you the assemblies; here you will learn the reasoning behind them, so you can judge any proposal a builder or insulation contractor puts in front of you.

Part 1The guide

Water you cannot see

Air is never completely dry. Even on a crisp winter day, the air inside a lived-in house carries water in the form of vapor, an invisible gas mixed with everything else you breathe. How much vapor air can hold depends on its temperature: warm air can carry a lot, cold air very little. Relative humidity, the number on a hygrometer, tells you how full the air is compared with what it could hold at that temperature.

When you cool a parcel of air without adding or removing any water, its relative humidity climbs, because the same amount of vapor is now squeezed into air that can hold less. Keep cooling it and you reach a point where the air is completely full. That temperature is the dew point. Below it, the extra vapor has nowhere to go and turns back into liquid water on the nearest surface.

This is the whole story of condensation, and it explains why metal buildings are special. Steel is a superb conductor of heat. A roof or wall panel takes on the outdoor temperature almost immediately, on both faces. On a 20°F night, the inside of a bare panel is not far from 20°F. Indoor air at 70°F and 40 percent humidity has a dew point in the mid-40s. The moment that air touches the steel, it is cooled far below its dew point and leaves its water behind.

Key ideaCondensation is not a defect of steel. It is what happens whenever humid air meets a surface colder than its dew point. The design goal is to make sure the two never meet.

Three ways moisture travels

Water reaches the places where it causes damage by three routes, and they are not equally important. Knowing the difference keeps you from spending money on the wrong fix.

Bulk water

Rain, snowmelt and ground water move as liquid, pulled by gravity and pushed by wind. They find edges, laps and holes. Bulk water is the most obvious source and the one builders think about first, which is why flashing, closures, gutters and grading appear on every competent set of drawings. Once these are right, liquid water from outside is rarely the cause of a problem inside a well-built barndominium.

Air leakage

This is the route most owners underestimate. Air moving through a gap carries its vapor with it. Building scientists have shown that a small crack can deliver many times more water into a wall or roof than the same area of solid material lets through by slow diffusion. A single unsealed electrical box, a gap around a ceiling light or a loose seam in insulation facing can feed a cold surface with moist air all winter. That is why the best insulation for living space is one that also stops air: closed-cell foam bonded to the panels, or insulated metal panels with sealed joints.

Diffusion

Vapor also moves slowly through materials themselves, from the humid side to the dry side. Materials that slow this movement are called vapor retarders. Diffusion matters, but it is gentle. It decides where a wall can safely dry, which is why the book warns against trapping a wall between two vapor barriers. A wall that gets a little wet and can dry is fine; a wall that gets a little wet and can never dry rots.

Key ideaStop the air first. In most barndominiums, sealing air paths solves more condensation than any extra layer of plastic.

Where the water comes from inside

A family of four adds a surprising amount of water to indoor air: breathing and perspiration, showers, cooking, dishwashing, houseplants, a pet bowl, laundry hung indoors. Together these can add several gallons of vapor every day. In an older, leaky farmhouse that vapor escapes through the gaps almost as fast as it is produced. A barndominium sealed with spray foam is a different animal. It holds on to that moisture, and indoor humidity keeps rising until something removes it.

There is also a one-time source that catches many new owners by surprise: the building itself. Concrete is mixed with far more water than the cement needs to harden, and a large slab gives off hundreds of gallons as it dries over its first months. Drywall mud, paint and wet framing add more. The first winter in a new barndominium is therefore the most humid it will ever be, and windows that stream with water in January of the first year are often a sign that ventilation is not running, not that the house is badly built.

Higher humidity raises the dew point, and a higher dew point turns more surfaces into condensation points. At 30 percent humidity a window frame may stay dry on a cold morning; at 60 percent the same frame is wet. That is why controlling indoor humidity is not a comfort detail. It is part of the building's moisture defense.

A quick comparisonAt 70°F indoors, air at 35 percent humidity has a dew point of about 41°F. At 55 percent it is about 53°F. That twelve-degree difference decides whether the corner of a closet on a north wall stays dry or starts to grow mold. Use the calculator at the end of this guide to try your own numbers.

Condensation or a leak? How to tell

When water appears inside, the first question is where it came from, because the fixes are completely different. A few observations answer it most of the time.

Look at the timing. Condensation follows temperature, not rain. Drips that appear on clear, cold mornings and stop by midday, or water that shows up after a cold snap with no precipitation at all, point to condensation. Water that appears during or just after rain, especially wind-driven rain from one direction, points to a leak.

Look at the pattern. Condensation tends to spread along cold elements: lines of drips following roof purlins, damp strips along wall girts, wet window frames all around the house at once. A leak usually has a single source and a trail you can follow upward, often to a fastener, a penetration, a lap or a piece of trim.

For a damp slab, there is a simple and very old test. Tape a square of clear plastic, about two feet on a side, tightly to the floor on all four edges and leave it for a day or two. If moisture collects under the plastic, it is coming up through the concrete from the ground. If it collects on top, the indoor air is too humid and the floor is acting as a cold surface. The first calls for a look at the vapor barrier and drainage; the second calls for ventilation and humidity control.

Finally, measure. A hygrometer that costs less than a pizza tells you the indoor humidity. If it reads above 55 percent in winter, start there before you cut into anything.

Choosing the defense for your house

With the physics in hand, the strategy is straightforward: keep the steel warm or keep humid air away from it, and give every part of the building a way to dry. The book compares insulation types in detail; here is how to think about the choice.

In heated living space, insist on a layer that does three things at once: insulates, stops air and controls vapor right against the steel. Closed-cell spray foam of about two inches or more does this, and so do insulated metal panels. Anything that relies on many separate pieces being perfectly taped and sealed, such as faced blankets or batts behind drywall, can work on paper but leaves you dependent on flawless installation that you will never see again once the walls are closed.

Pay attention to the steel members, not just the panels. Purlins, girts and columns are thermal bridges: they carry cold through the insulation layer. Foam that covers the panels and the inside face of the framing removes those cold lines; insulation placed only between them leaves a grid of cold steel waiting for humid air.

Think about your climate. In cold regions the vapor drive is from the warm inside toward the cold outside, so the interior must be airtight. In hot, humid regions the drive reverses for much of the year, and humid outdoor air is the threat; an airtight envelope and dehumidification matter more than any interior vapor barrier. In mixed climates the direction changes with the season, which is exactly why a single plastic sheet behind drywall is risky there. Ask your designer which climate zone your county is in before you approve the insulation details.

Separate the shop. An attached shop that is heated only now and then, washed down, or used to park wet vehicles produces a lot of moisture and has large cold surfaces. Keep its air separate from the house air with a sealed wall and door, give it its own ventilation, and insulate it at least enough that the roof steel does not drip on your tools.

Living in the house: the first year and after

Moisture control does not end at the final inspection. A barndominium is a system, and the people inside run the most important part of it.

Start the ventilation on the first day and leave it running. If you have an ERV or HRV, learn how to set it and clean its filters. Use bath fans during every shower and for twenty minutes afterward; a humidity-sensing switch does this for you. Run the range hood whenever you boil or fry. Vent the dryer outdoors and keep its duct clean and short.

Watch the numbers through the first winter. Keep indoor humidity roughly between 30 and 50 percent, toward the low end when it is very cold outside. If it stays high despite the fans, the house needs more ventilation or a dehumidifier, and it is far cheaper to add one now than to replace stained drywall later.

Make a seasonal walk-around a habit: gutters and downspouts in autumn, window corners and closets on the coldest mornings, roof penetrations and trim in spring, humidity during long humid spells in summer. The checklist in Part 2 of this guide turns that routine into a page you can print and keep with your house documents.

Treat any water you find as information. A single wet morning after a party for thirty people is normal. Water that comes back every cold morning in the same place is the building telling you exactly where humid air is reaching cold steel. Find the path, seal it, and the problem usually ends there.

Questions to ask before you sign

Before you approve drawings or sign with an insulation contractor, ask these questions and make sure the answers appear in writing. What insulation goes on the roof and walls of each space, and at what total thickness? Is the steel framing covered, or only the panels? Where exactly is the air barrier, and who is responsible for sealing it at the slab, the eaves and around openings? Is there any plastic vapor barrier on the interior side, and why? How will the house be ventilated, and was the air conditioner sized with a load calculation? How is the shop separated from the house?

A good builder answers these without hesitation. Vague answers, or a promise that the house will simply breathe, are a signal to slow down and ask for details before work starts.

Humidity from inside the house

A family adds several gallons of water vapor a day by breathing, cooking, showering and drying clothes. A tight, foamed barndominium keeps that water unless you remove it on purpose. Aim for 30 to 50 percent relative humidity indoors, lower in very cold weather.

Typical mistakeRunning a tight house with no ventilation through its first winter. New concrete and drywall release moisture for months; without fresh air, windows stream with water every morning. Turn on the ventilation from day one.

Seasonal check, once the house is built

  • AutumnClean gutters, check downspout extensions, look along the base of the walls for splash damage, and confirm the bath fans still run quietly.
  • WinterOn the coldest mornings, look at window corners, closet back walls and the underside of any exposed steel. Any water means humid air is reaching a cold surface.
  • SpringWalk the roof edges and penetrations from a ladder or with binoculars; reseal any boot or trim that has lifted.
  • SummerIn humid climates, check the indoor humidity during long rainy spells and run a dehumidifier if it stays above 55 percent.
