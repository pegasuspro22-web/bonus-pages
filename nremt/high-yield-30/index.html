Scene Size-Up & Safety

Q1START triage
After a stage collapse at an outdoor concert, you begin triaging several adults with START. One man is not walking, is breathing 24 times a minute, and has a strong radial pulse. When you ask him to show you two fingers, he mumbles unrelated words and does not respond to your command. Which triage category applies?
Correct answer: C. START checks walking, breathing, perfusion, and mental status in that order. His breathing rate is under 30 and his radial pulse is present, but a patient who cannot follow a simple command fails the mental-status check and is tagged Immediate. Delayed would fit only if he obeyed commands. Minor is reserved for patients who can walk. Deceased/expectant applies to patients who are not breathing even after you open the airway.
Q2Hazmat awareness
You are dispatched to a chemical storage building for a worker who is lightheaded and vomiting. A coworker says he is inside. Outside a partly open bay door you see a diamond-shaped placard with a skull and crossbones on several drums, and you smell a sharp chemical odor. What should you do first?
Correct answer: A. A placard and a strong odor point to a hazardous-materials scene, and EMTs at the awareness level do not enter it. Stay uphill and upwind, keep people out, and request trained hazmat resources. Sending the coworker in adds a second victim and can spread contamination. Holding your breath does not protect your skin or eyes. Entering first and calling later puts you and your crew at risk.
Q3Violent scene
Your crew is sent to a nightclub parking lot for a man with a stab wound. Bystanders say the attacker ran off but disagree about which way he went, and police have not arrived. The patient is calling for help about 30 feet away. What is the best action?
Correct answer: D. Scene safety comes before patient care, and an unsecured scene with an unknown attacker is not safe. Staging until law enforcement clears it protects your crew so you can help this patient and others. Driving around looking for the attacker puts you in the danger you are trying to avoid. Splitting the crew leaves one person watching for a threat without any protection. A fast rush in treats the patient but ignores the risk of adding two more patients.
Q4PPE selection
An agitated patient with a bleeding mouth injury after a fall is spitting blood and thrashing as you try to hold his head still. You expect to suction and to have close body contact. Which PPE is most appropriate?
Correct answer: B. Choose PPE for every route of exposure the call creates. Spitting and suctioning risk blood in the eyes, nose, and mouth, and close contact risks soaking your uniform, so full protection is needed. Gloves alone leave the face and clothing exposed. Skipping the mask ignores splash to the mouth and nose. Leaving off gloves removes the most basic barrier when you are handling blood.
Q5Scene hazards
You respond to a building site where a worker was struck by a falling pipe. The patient is lying beneath scaffolding while a crane is still lifting materials directly overhead. What is the safest initial action?
Correct answer: A. An active overhead hazard makes the scene unsafe, so it has to be controlled before you enter. Ask the site supervisor to stop the crane and secure the area. Rushing in with an eye on the load still exposes you to a second strike. A scaffold roof is not built to stop falling objects and can create false confidence. Waiting for an injured patient to crawl out delays care and can worsen injuries.
Section 2 of 5 · 13 questions

Primary Assessment

Q6Pediatric general impression
A 2-year-old is brought to you after choking on a piece of hot dog. The child is now breathing. Which finding from your first look is most concerning?
Correct answer: D. Your first look uses the pediatric assessment triangle: appearance, work of breathing, and color of the skin. A quiet, limp child with blue lips has poor tone and poor oxygenation, which signals a serious problem. Loud crying, screaming at a stranger, and pushing your hands away all show a child who is awake, moving air, and reacting normally.
Q7General impression changes
You enter a kitchen where a 72-year-old man with diabetes is seated at the table. He greets you and answers your first question. Seconds later he stares blankly, picks at his shirt, and no longer squeezes your hand when asked. His breathing is regular and his skin is pale and sweaty. What is the best conclusion about his general impression?
Correct answer: B. General impression is not a single snapshot. It continues while you observe. A patient who loses the ability to follow commands and becomes pale and sweaty is deteriorating and needs urgent attention, whatever the cause turns out to be. His first answer does not cancel what you see next. Blaming age delays care. History matters, but only after you deal with a changing mental status.
Q8AVPU
A 22-year-old is found on a couch after suspected drug use. When you speak loudly, he briefly opens his eyes and mumbles, then drifts off again. Slow breathing is present. Which AVPU level best describes his responsiveness?
Correct answer: C. He reacts when you speak, so he is at the Verbal level of AVPU, even though the response is brief and weak. Alert would require him to be awake and oriented without being prompted. Pain would mean he responds only to a painful stimulus, and unresponsive means no response at all. A Verbal level with slow breathing marks him as a high-priority patient.
Q9Oropharyngeal airway
You insert an oropharyngeal airway in an unresponsive adult after a fall from a ladder. As soon as it is in place, the patient gags and retches. What should you do next?
Correct answer: A. An oropharyngeal airway is for patients with no gag reflex. Gagging means the reflex is present and the device can trigger vomiting and aspiration, so remove it. Then keep suction ready and manage the airway with positioning, choosing another adjunct only if it suits the patient's injuries. Pushing it deeper or taping it in place forces a device the patient cannot tolerate, and gagging is not a sign of a good airway.
Q10Inadequate breathing
A 65-year-old man with COPD is on oxygen by nonrebreather mask. Over several minutes he becomes drowsy, his breathing rate falls from 26 to 8 per minute, and his chest rise is barely visible. What is the priority?
Correct answer: D. A slow, shallow breathing rate with drowsiness means breathing is now inadequate and he needs assisted ventilations right away. Withholding or cutting oxygen from a hypoxic patient is the more dangerous mistake, because the concern about suppressing his drive does not outweigh low oxygen. Waiting ten minutes wastes time he does not have. Coaching only works if he is alert enough to cooperate, and he is not.
Q11Oxygen delivery
A patient with shortness of breath is receiving oxygen through a nonrebreather mask. Each time the patient breathes in, the reservoir bag flattens completely. What is the most likely cause?
Correct answer: B. A nonrebreather works only if the reservoir stays inflated, so the patient breathes from a full bag. A bag that collapses on every breath usually means the flow is too low, so raise it (typically 10 to 15 liters per minute) and check the setup. A collapsing bag says nothing about recovery, and it is not a sign of excess oxygen or full lungs.
Q12Bleeding control
A landscaper cuts his lower leg with a chainsaw. Blood is pulsing from the wound and soaks through the dressing you applied with firm direct pressure. What is the best next step?
Correct answer: C. Life-threatening extremity bleeding that direct pressure does not control calls for a tourniquet placed above the wound, tightened until bleeding stops, with the time written down. Elevation and pressure alone have already failed. Cold does not stop arterial bleeding. Removing the dressing disturbs any clot that has formed and wastes time while he keeps losing blood.
Q13Shock recognition
A patient with suspected internal bleeding after a motorcycle crash is being transported. He is anxious and pale, and his heart rate is rising. At reassessment, which finding most strongly suggests he is getting worse?
Correct answer: A. Falling mental status and weakening peripheral pulses are late signs that his body can no longer compensate for blood loss. Unchanged pain does not show a change in perfusion. A calmer patient who answers clearly is better, not worse, and a heart rate that settles after reassurance points away from decompensation.
Q14BVM effectiveness
After an opioid overdose, an adult is not breathing adequately and his oxygen saturation is 85%. You start ventilating with a bag-mask and oxygen. What is the best sign that each squeeze is working?
Correct answer: D. Visible, even chest rise is the most reliable sign that air is reaching the lungs. Air escaping around the mask means a poor seal. A swelling abdomen means air is entering the stomach, which risks vomiting. A bag that feels soft and refills instantly usually signals a leak, not a good ventilation.
Q15BVM resistance
You are ventilating a patient in severe respiratory distress with a bag-mask device and oxygen. Over several minutes the bag becomes harder to squeeze and chest rise drops, even though the mask seal and head position are good and you have suctioned the mouth. What should you suspect?
Correct answer: B. When technique is right and the airway is clear, a bag that gets harder to squeeze points to a problem in the lungs, such as bronchospasm or a stiffening lung. That is a sign of serious deterioration and needs rapid transport and updated information to the hospital. Resistance does not mean improvement. Cylinder pressure does not change how hard the bag is to squeeze, and a pulse oximeter cannot explain what you feel in your hands.
Q16Tracheostomy suctioning
A 63-year-old man with a home tracheostomy is short of breath. Thick secretions are visible at the opening and his breathing is noisy. What is most appropriate if it is within your training and protocol?
Correct answer: A. Noisy breathing with visible secretions at a tracheostomy suggests a partial blockage, and clearing it with suction is the right step. The stoma may be the patient's only airway, so sealing it or removing the tube is dangerous. Ventilating only through the mouth ignores the actual route the air takes.
Q17CPR
During transport of a patient with chest pressure, the patient slumps, becomes unresponsive, and is not breathing normally. You cannot feel a carotid pulse within 10 seconds. What should you do next?
Correct answer: C. An unresponsive patient who is not breathing normally and has no pulse is in cardiac arrest, and every second without compressions lowers survival. Start CPR (adult compressions at 100 to 120 per minute, at least 2 inches deep, minimal pauses) and apply the AED as soon as it is available. A blood pressure is not a priority now, extra checking only delays treatment, and transport speed does not replace compressions.
Q18Airway burns
A man is pulled from a kitchen fire with soot around his nose and mouth. He is still talking, but his voice becomes hoarse and you hear noisy breathing when he inhales. Oxygen is in place. What should you anticipate?
Correct answer: B. Soot, a hoarse voice, and noisy inhalation suggest heat or smoke injury to the airway, where swelling can close the airway quickly even when the patient is still talking. Prepare for worse breathing and move fast. Drinking is unsafe with a threatened airway. Oxygen does not reduce swelling, and dismissing the signs as agitation misses a life threat.

Secondary Assessment

Q19OPQRST
A 61-year-old man has had chest pressure for half an hour and speaks clearly. You are gathering his symptom history with OPQRST. Which question addresses radiation?
Correct answer: D. The R stands for radiation, so ask whether the discomfort moves anywhere, such as to the arm, jaw, or back. What he was doing when it began is Onset, what it feels like is Quality, and the time of his last meal belongs to the SAMPLE history, not to OPQRST.
Q20Distal circulation
A stable teenager has a swollen, deformed forearm after falling from a bike. Which finding most strongly suggests compromised circulation below the injury?
Correct answer: C. A pale, cool hand with no pulse means blood is not reaching the limb below the injury, which needs prompt attention. Severe pain is common with fractures and does not show what the circulation is doing. Not recalling the time of the fall is a history detail, and warmth is not a sign of lost circulation.
Section 4 of 5 · 7 questions

Patient Treatment & Transport

Q21Oral glucose
A pale, sweaty, confused woman in her 70s with a history of diabetes is sitting in a chair. She can swallow safely and obeys simple commands, and you have oral glucose with you. What is your first action?
Correct answer: A. A patient with diabetes who has classic signs of low blood sugar, who is awake and able to swallow and follow commands, should receive oral glucose. Plain water first wastes time. Prone positioning is unsafe and does not help. Confusion is a common sign of low blood sugar, not a reason to rule it out.
Q22Nitroglycerin criteria
You are evaluating a 57-year-old man with chest pressure and a nitroglycerin prescription in his pocket. He is alert. Which of these findings means you should hold the medication and call medical direction?
Correct answer: D. Nitroglycerin lowers blood pressure, so a low pressure such as 84/50 is a reason to hold it. Taking aspirin does not rule it out, and exertional onset and severe pain fit cardiac chest pain and support treatment, not withholding. Also confirm he has not used an erectile-dysfunction medication recently, which is another reason to withhold.
Q23Asthma in children
A 9-year-old girl with asthma is struggling to breathe. Early on she sits upright and wheezes loudly. During transport the wheezes fade, her chest hardly rises, and she grows sleepy. How should you interpret this change?
Correct answer: B. Quieter wheezing is only good if breathing has eased. When faint sounds come with poor chest rise and drowsiness, she is moving too little air and is heading toward respiratory failure, so prepare to assist ventilations and give the hospital an update. The other explanations mistake exhaustion for improvement or calm.
Q24Epinephrine reassessment
A woman develops hives, wheezing, and lip swelling after a bee sting. Her epinephrine auto-injector was used before you arrived. Five minutes later she says she is breathing easier. What is the best next step?
Correct answer: C. Epinephrine can wear off before the reaction is over, so an allergic reaction can worsen again. Continue oxygen, keep watching her airway and breathing, and transport for further care. Treating symptoms as anxiety, stopping monitoring, and canceling transport all assume the danger has passed.
Q25Stroke reassessment
A 68-year-old woman with sudden facial droop and slurred speech is being transported to a stroke center. On the way she becomes more confused and vomits. What is the best interpretation?
Correct answer: D. New confusion and vomiting in a possible stroke suggest her condition is getting worse. Reassess airway and breathing, be ready to suction, keep transport rapid, and update the receiving hospital. Fatigue, improvement from vomiting, and an expected oxygen effect all downplay a possible deterioration.
Q26Burn care
A cook has a scald burn with intact blisters on his forearm and hand from boiling water. The burn was cooled briefly at the scene. What is most appropriate during transport?
Correct answer: A. After brief cooling, a clean, dry dressing protects the burn from contamination. Intact blisters act as a barrier, so leave them alone. Thick ointment traps heat and has to be removed later, and ice can damage tissue further and cool the patient too much.
Q27Newborn airway
Moments after delivery, a full-term newborn is crying strongly and moving all extremities, but thick secretions are blocking the mouth and nose. What should you do for the airway?
Correct answer: B. A newborn with a strong cry and good tone does not need compressions. The problem is blocked airways, so clear them gently with a bulb syringe, mouth first, then the nose. Waiting leaves the obstruction in place, and prolonged rubbing does not clear secretions.
Section 5 of 5 · 3 questions

Operations

Q28DNR documentation
An aide at a nursing home tells you a resident being sent to the hospital "has a DNR," but the chart holds no signed form and no order can be found. The resident is awake and breathing comfortably. What should you do before leaving the facility?
Correct answer: C. A DNR is honored on the basis of proper documentation under local rules. Verbal reports from staff or family are not the order itself. Ask for the paperwork, and if it cannot be produced, follow your local protocol, which usually means treating the patient as a full-code patient. Refusing transport until a physician arrives delays care for a stable patient.
Q29Documentation
The tablet you use for your patient care report stops responding en route and will not save what you typed. Your stable patient needs no urgent care. What should you do?
Correct answer: A. Documentation must be timely and accurate, and it is part of the legal record. A backup method such as paper keeps the details from being lost, and you transfer them into the report as soon as possible. Waiting until the end of the shift risks forgetting information. Making the patient repeat details is disruptive, and ending the transport for a technical problem puts a patient's care second.
Q30Lifting and moving
You and your partner respond to a bariatric patient with weakness who cannot walk. The patient is stable, the hallway is narrow, and you doubt two people can move him safely. What should you do first?
Correct answer: D. A stable patient gives you time to plan a safe move. Calling for more people or bariatric equipment protects both the patient and your backs. Attempting it alone risks dropping the patient or injuring the crew. Family members are not trained to lift, and asking a weak patient to crawl is unsafe.
