Reading

PassageMs. Ortiz noticed that during independent reading time, several students kept asking her to define words instead of trying to figure them out first. She began asking, “What do you think it means? What clues does the sentence give you?” before offering the definition herself. Within a few weeks, more students started using context clues on their own before asking for help.
Q1Main Idea
What is the main idea of the passage?
Correct answer: D. The passage shows Ms. Ortiz shifting her approach — asking guiding questions about context clues before giving definitions — which led students to try that strategy on their own. That shift is the passage's main idea. Choice A misstates what happened: she still helped, just differently. Choice B overgeneralizes a specific teaching strategy into an absolute rule the passage never states. Choice C introduces a detail that never appears in the passage.
Q2Vocabulary in Context
As used in the passage, “context clues” most nearly means:
Correct answer: B. “Context clues” refers to hints within the surrounding sentence or passage that help a reader figure out an unfamiliar word's meaning — exactly what Ms. Ortiz was prompting students to notice (“What clues does the sentence give you?”). Choice A confuses context clues with spelling rules, an unrelated skill. Choice C describes a vocabulary list, not a reading strategy. Choice D describes a dictionary definition, which is the opposite of figuring a word out independently from context.
PassageA classroom library was organized by reading level, but many students always picked the same few books from the shelf. The paraeducator added a rotating display of new titles at students' eye level each Monday. Within a month, checkout records showed a wider variety of books being borrowed than before.
Q3Main Idea
What is the main idea of the passage?
Correct answer: A. The passage describes a specific change (a rotating display of new titles) and its measured effect (a wider variety of books borrowed) — that cause-and-effect relationship is the main idea. Choice B is not supported; the passage never says reading levels were removed. Choice C contradicts the passage, which says students did pick books, just a narrow set at first. Choice D introduces a detail never mentioned.
Q4Supporting Evidence
Which detail from the passage best supports the idea that the display was effective?
Correct answer: C. The checkout records showing a wider variety of borrowed books are the direct evidence the passage gives that the change worked. Choice A describes the library's original organization, not evidence of the display's effect. Choice B describes the problem before the change, not evidence that the solution worked. Choice D describes a detail of how the display was set up, not evidence of its outcome.
Q5Vocabulary in Context
In the sentence “The teacher's instructions were concise, so the students finished quickly,” the word “concise” most nearly means:
Correct answer: D. “Concise” means expressed in few words, clearly and briefly — consistent with instructions that let students finish quickly. Choice A is close to the opposite; confusing instructions would likely slow students down, not speed them up. Choice B introduces a meaning unrelated to “concise.” Choice C is also nearly opposite — repeated instructions would take longer, not less time.
Q6Vocabulary in Context
In the sentence “The librarian's approach to organizing the shelves was methodical, following the same careful steps every time,” the word “methodical” most nearly means:
Correct answer: A. “Methodical” describes an orderly, systematic way of doing something, matching “following the same careful steps every time.” Choice B is the opposite of methodical. Choice C misreads “every time” in the sentence, which indicates repetition, not a single occurrence. Choice D contradicts the idea of following careful, deliberate steps.
Q7Instructional Application
A paraeducator notices that a student can read most words on a page correctly but cannot explain what the passage was about afterward. Which strategy would best address this specific difficulty?
Correct answer: B. The student's difficulty is with comprehension, not word recognition, so pausing to summarize builds the specific skill of tracking meaning while reading. Choice A addresses speed, not comprehension, and would not target the actual problem. Choice C increases difficulty without addressing the comprehension gap, likely making the problem worse. Choice D avoids the passage entirely and shifts to an unrelated skill, not the comprehension strategy the student needs.
Q8Instructional Application
A student consistently mispronounces multisyllabic words by guessing based on the first letter alone. Which approach would most directly help the student improve?
Correct answer: D. Breaking words into syllable chunks directly targets the student's specific error (guessing from the first letter) by teaching a decoding strategy for the whole word. Choice A provides the answer but does not teach a transferable skill the student can use independently next time. Choice B avoids the problem rather than building the skill needed to solve it. Choice C (repetition without a new strategy) does not address the underlying decoding error.
PassageBefore the class field trip, the teacher reviewed the schedule three times and posted it on the board. On the day of the trip, only two students asked what time the bus would leave. The teacher noted this was far fewer questions than in previous years, when the schedule had only been announced once verbally.
Q9Main Idea
What is the main idea of the passage?
Correct answer: C. The passage links the repeated, posted schedule to a measurable drop in questions compared to previous years — that connection is the main idea. Choice A is not supported; the passage never suggests canceling trips. Choice B introduces a detail never mentioned. Choice D contradicts the passage, since students did ask about the schedule, just fewer of them.
Q10Inference
Based on the passage, what can be reasonably inferred about how the schedule was communicated in previous years?
Correct answer: A. The passage directly contrasts this year's approach (“reviewed... three times and posted”) with previous years, when “the schedule had only been announced once verbally” — meaning it was not posted and was communicated only a single time. Choice B contradicts the direct contrast the passage draws. Choice C goes further than the passage supports; it says the schedule was announced, just less thoroughly. Choice D introduces a method the passage never mentions.
Section 2 of 3 · 10 questions

Mathematics

Q11Fractions
A recipe calls for 2/3 cup of sugar. If the recipe is doubled, how much sugar is needed?
Correct answer: B. Doubling 2/3 gives 2/3 × 2 = 4/3 cup (1 1/3 cups). Choice A results from halving the amount instead of doubling it. Choice C results from the common error of adding the numerators and denominators separately (2+2=4, 3+3=6) instead of multiplying the numerator only. Choice D results from tripling the amount instead of doubling it: 2/3 × 3 = 2.
Q12Percent
A paraeducator scored 42 out of 50 points on a training quiz. What percent did the paraeducator score?
Correct answer: C. 42/50 = 0.84 = 84%. Choice A results from misreading the total as 60 instead of 50: 42/60 = 70%. Choice B results from misreading the score as 40 instead of 42: 40/50 = 80%. Choice D results from subtracting the missed points from 100 without converting them to a percent of 50 first: 100 − (50−42) = 100 − 8 = 92.
Q13Decimals
A paraeducator has $18.75 and spends $6.40 on classroom supplies. How much money is left?
Correct answer: A. $18.75 − $6.40 = $12.35. Choice B results from subtracting $7.40 instead of $6.40, a $1 misread of the amount spent: 18.75−7.40=11.35. Choice C results from misreading $6.40 as $6.30: 18.75−6.30=12.45. Choice D results from adding the amount spent instead of subtracting it: 18.75+6.40=25.15.
Q14Algebra
Solve for x: 2x + 5 = 17
Correct answer: D. Subtracting 5 from both sides gives 2x=12; dividing by 2 gives x=6. Choice A results from subtracting the coefficient (2) from 12 instead of dividing by it: 12−2=10. Choice B results from adding 5 to 17 instead of subtracting it: 2x=22, x=11. Choice C is the value of 2x itself (17−5=12), mistakenly left un-divided by 2.
Q15Geometry
A rectangular classroom rug measures 6 feet by 4 feet. What is its perimeter?
Correct answer: B. Perimeter = 2×(6+4) = 20 feet. Choice A adds the two side lengths (6+4=10) but forgets to double the sum for the two pairs of sides. Choice C computes the rug's area (6×4=24) instead of its perimeter. Choice D results from doubling the area (24×2=48) instead of correctly computing the perimeter.
Q16Data Analysis
A paraeducator recorded how many books each of 5 students read in a month: Ana–4, Ben–6, Cleo–3, Dev–6, Ella–5. What is the median number of books read?
Correct answer: C. Sorted from least to greatest: 3, 4, 5, 6, 6. The median is the middle value: 5. Choice A results from picking the second-lowest value instead of the true middle value after sorting. Choice B is the mode (6 appears twice), not the median. Choice D is the sum of all five values (4+6+3+6+5=24), not the median.
Q17Ratio & Proportion
In a classroom, the ratio of boys to girls is 3 to 5. If there are 12 boys, how many girls are there?
Correct answer: A. The scale factor is 12÷3=4, so girls = 5×4=20. Choice B results from adding the boys' ratio number (3) directly to the boy count instead of finding and applying the scale factor: 12+3=15. Choice C results from adding the girls' ratio number (5) directly to the boy count instead of scaling proportionally: 12+5=17. Choice D results from subtracting the girls' ratio number (5) from the boy count instead of scaling proportionally: 12−5=7.
Q18Order of Operations
What is the value of 3 + 2 × (8 − 5)?
Correct answer: B. Following order of operations: parentheses first (8−5=3), then multiplication (2×3=6), then addition (3+6=9). Choice A results from adding 3 and 2 first (5), then multiplying by 3: 5×3=15. Choice C results from ignoring the parentheses and evaluating strictly in the order written: 2×8=16, then 3+16−5=14. Choice D results from dropping the “3+” term entirely and computing only 2×(8−5)=6.
Q19Diagnosing Student Errors
A student solved 1/4 + 2/4 and wrote the answer as 3/8. What error did the student most likely make?
Correct answer: D. The answer 3/8 comes exactly from adding the numerators (1+2=3) and the denominators (4+4=8) separately, instead of keeping the common denominator and adding only the numerators (which gives 3/4). Choice A is incorrect because subtracting the fractions would give 1/4, not 3/8. Choice B is incorrect because multiplying the fractions would give 2/16 (1/8), not 3/8. Choice C is incorrect because converting to decimals and adding would give 0.75, not a value matching 3/8 (0.375).
Q20Diagnosing Student Errors
A student rounded 452 to the nearest hundred and wrote 400. What error did the student most likely make?
Correct answer: C. 452 is closer to 500 than to 400 (only 48 away from 500, versus 52 away from 400), so the correct rounded value is 500. The student's answer of 400 means they rounded down instead of up. Choice A is incorrect because rounding 452 to the nearest ten gives 450, not 400. Choice B is incorrect because adding 100 to 452 gives 552, not 400. Choice D is incorrect because rounding 452 to the nearest thousand gives 0, not 400.

Segment A incorrectly uses the subject pronoun “I” as the object of the preposition “between.” Prepositions take object pronouns, so the correct form is “between you and me.” Segment B is correct. Segment C is correct. Since segment A contains a genuine error, “No mistakes” is incorrect.
Q23Punctuation

Correct answer: C. Segment C creates a comma splice: “...labeled each folder clearly, this made it easier...” joins two independent clauses with only a comma. Q24Capitalization
Identify the segment that contains an error. If there is no error, choose “No mistakes.”
Correct answer: B. Segment B fails to capitalize “tuesday.” Days of the week are proper nouns and must always be capitalized: “Tuesday.” Segment A is correct — “principal” is used generically here with no name following it, so it should not be capitalized. Q25Sentence Structure
Which is the best way to combine these two sentences?
“The paraeducator prepared the materials early. The lesson could start on time.”
Correct answer: D. Choice D clearly shows the purpose relationship between the two ideas using “so that” — the materials were prepared for the purpose of starting the lesson on time. Choice A creates a comma splice by joining two independent clauses with only a comma. Choice B uses “but,” a contrast word, which misrepresents the relationship — there is no contrast between the two ideas. Choice C is awkward and unnecessarily wordy (“it was the lesson that”), which weakens clarity instead of improving it.
Q26Paragraph Organization
Which sentence does NOT belong in the following paragraph?
“(1) The after-school program offers homework help every day. (2) Students can also join a chess club on Wednesdays. (3) The school's cafeteria serves lunch at noon. (4) On Fridays, there is an art workshop for students who want to draw or paint.”
Correct answer: A. Sentences 1, 2, and 4 all describe activities offered by the after-school program, which is the paragraph's topic. Sentence 3, about when the cafeteria serves lunch, is unrelated to the after-school program's activities and breaks the paragraph's focus. Choices B, C, and D are incorrect because those sentences all directly support the paragraph's main topic.
Q27Writing Process
A student has finished writing a first draft of a story. According to the standard writing process, what should the student do next?
Correct answer: C. In the standard writing process (prewriting, drafting, revising, editing, publishing), revising follows drafting — the writer reviews and improves the draft's content and organization before moving on to editing and publishing. Choice A skips essential steps in the process. Choice B abandons the current piece instead of continuing the process with it. Choice D treats the first draft as a finished product, skipping revision and editing entirely.
Q28Spelling
Identify the segment that contains an error. If there is no error, choose “No mistakes.”
Correct answer: A. Segment A misspells “separate” (correct spelling has an “a” in the middle: s-e-p-a-r-a-t-e, not “seperate”). Segments B and C contain no spelling errors. Because segment A contains a clear spelling error, “No mistakes” is incorrect.
Q29Punctuation
Identify the segment that contains an error. If there is no error, choose “No mistakes.”
Correct answer: D. The sentence is correctly constructed throughout: segment A closes the introductory clause with a comma, segment B is a correctly formed independent clause with proper subject-verb-pronoun agreement (“students... their”), and segment C completes the sentence with a parallel verb phrase joined by “and” without an unnecessary comma. Q30Instructional Application
A student's paragraph begins: “There are many things about recess. It is fun. Some kids play tag.” The teacher wants the student to add a clear topic sentence. Which revision best achieves this?
Correct answer: B. Choice B clearly states the paragraph's main idea (recess being enjoyable because of games) and gives the paragraph a clear focus, which is what a topic sentence should do. Choice A is vague — “many things” does not tell the reader what the paragraph is actually about. Choice C and Choice D are supporting details, not topic sentences; they describe specific parts of recess rather than introducing the paragraph's overall focus.
