Questions 801–900

Question 801

The CMA receives an electronic message from a patient portal stating, “I think my antibiotic is causing a rash—should I stop it?”; what should the CMA do first?

Treat as a clinical concern: assess for red flags per protocol, notify the provider promptly, and document the report because medication advice requires provider direction and rash may indicate allergy (common trap: telling the patient to stop/start meds independently).
Question 802

A patient’s spouse asks in the waiting room, “So what did the doctor say it is?” while the patient is in the restroom; what should the CMA do?

Do not discuss the patient’s condition and instead say you can speak when the patient is present/authorizes it because even casual conversation in public areas can violate HIPAA (common trap: assuming family present equals permission).
Question 803

A patient calls to dispute a bill stating, “I was charged for a level 4 visit but it was quick”; what should the CMA do next?

Route the concern to billing/coding and document the complaint because CMAs should not change codes and coding depends on documented complexity, not visit length (common trap: promising a code change to satisfy the patient).
Question 804

A new patient brings a handwritten list of medications and says, “Just put this in my chart”; what should the CMA do to ensure accurate documentation?

Perform medication reconciliation by clarifying drug name, strength, directions, and OTC/herbals, then document as patient-reported and flag discrepancies for the provider because copying incomplete lists leads to dangerous errors (common trap: entering meds without dose or frequency).
Question 805

At check-in, a patient asks you to confirm whether her coworker “was here last week for an STI test,” and she insists she’s just trying to help; what should the CMA do next?

Politely refuse to confirm or deny the coworker’s visit and offer to take a message for the coworker to contact the clinic, because HIPAA prohibits disclosing any patient’s presence or services without authorization (a common trap is thinking “just saying yes/no” isn’t PHI).
Question 806

At check-in, a patient says their spouse can’t remember the portal password and asks you to “just tell him my lab results over the phone” while the patient is standing at the window; what should the CMA do next?

Verify who is requesting the information and obtain the patient’s explicit permission using a HIPAA-compliant process (and document it) before any disclosure, because sharing results without confirming identity/authorization is a common HIPAA breach even if the patient is present and insists.
Question 807

The CMA documents an immunization but accidentally selects the wrong lot number in the EHR; the patient is still in the clinic; what should the CMA do next?

Correct the lot number immediately using the EHR’s approved correction method and notify the supervisor if required because lot tracking affects recalls and patient safety (common trap: waiting until end of day and risking inaccurate inventory/records).
Question 808

A patient asks to amend their record saying, “Remove the note about substance use—it’s embarrassing”; what should the CMA do next?

Explain the amendment process and route the request to the designated records/EHR workflow because patients can request amendments but entries are not simply deleted from the legal record (common trap: agreeing to erase documentation on request).
Question 809

A patient calls asking for their lab results and says, “I’m the spouse; we share everything,” and you see no release on file; what is the best response?

Explain you can only discuss results with the patient (or an authorized contact) and offer to speak directly with the patient or have them add permission, because relationship alone is not authorization and the trap is “verifying identity” but still disclosing without consent.
Question 810

You receive a faxed request from a law office for a patient’s full chart with no patient authorization attached; what should you do next?

Do not release records; route it to the designated records/privacy process and request proper authorization/subpoena review, because legal requests require verification and the trap is sending “because it looks official.”
Question 811

A provider gives a verbal order in the hallway to “refill her blood pressure med,” and the patient is standing there waiting; what should the CMA do?

Tell the provider you will route the request through the proper process for the provider to authorize (no refills by CMA) and document/forward the message, because medication prescribing is outside scope and the trap is treating a “verbal order” as permission to act.
Question 812

A parent insists on staying in the room during an adult patient’s visit and answers all questions for them; what is the best CMA action?

Ask the parent to step out briefly to confirm the patient’s preferences and obtain consent to share information, because the patient controls disclosure and the trap is assuming “family” equals permission.
Question 813

A patient’s after-visit summary prints at the front desk where other patients are standing; what is the best immediate step?

Retrieve the document and move it to a secure area before handing it to the patient, because printed PHI must be protected and the trap is leaving it “just for a moment.”
Question 814

A 16-year-old arrives alone for STI testing and asks that their parent not be told; what should the CMA do first?

Bring the patient to a private area and alert the provider/office lead to follow state minor-consent and confidentiality rules, because privacy may be legally protected and the trap is automatically calling the parent without checking applicable consent laws.
Question 815

A coworker asks you to “quickly look up” a neighbor’s appointment time in the EHR because they’re “worried”; what should you do?

Refuse and remind them access must be job-related, offering to connect them with the proper channel if there’s a safety concern, because viewing records without need-to-know is a HIPAA violation and the trap is “just checking one thing.”
Question 816

While rooming a patient, you notice yesterday’s visit note in the EHR contains a medication dose that conflicts with the printed prescription the provider signed; the patient is asking you to “fix it in the chart” before leaving—what should you do next?

Do not alter the provider’s note; promptly notify the provider and document the discrepancy using an addendum/correction process with date/time and your name so the record stays accurate and auditable, avoiding the common trap of editing someone else’s entry or “making it match” without provider review.
Question 817

At check-in, a patient’s spouse asks you to “print her lab results from last week” while the patient is in the restroom and the spouse says “she won’t mind,” what should the CMA do next?

Do not release or print the results and instead ask to speak with the patient to obtain her authorization (or confirm documented permission) because HIPAA requires verification before disclosing PHI, and a common trap is assuming a spouse automatically has access.
Question 818

A claim is denied for “incorrect patient date of birth,” and the patient is calling angry; what should the CMA do next?

Verify the DOB in the EHR against ID/insurance and route correction/rebill steps to the billing workflow, because many denials are registration errors and the trap is blaming the insurer without checking demographics.
Question 819

The provider documents a diagnosis after the visit, and the patient’s follow-up appointment needs to match the recommended timeframe; what should the CMA do?

Schedule based on the provider’s documented plan (and clarify with the provider if unclear), because scheduling must align with medical necessity and the trap is choosing a convenient slot that delays care.
Question 820

A patient is scheduled for a procedure visit but arrives 20 minutes late, the provider is double-booked, and the patient is upset; what is the best CMA action?

Use de-escalation and offer the realistic options (wait, reschedule, or convert to a shorter visit if approved), coordinating with the provider/front desk, because scheduling changes require team coordination and the trap is promising they’ll be seen “right away.”
Question 821

A patient calls stating they are suicidal and asks for “someone to talk to,” and the provider is with another patient; what should the CMA do first?

Keep the patient on the line, gather location/callback number, and immediately alert a supervisor/provider to initiate emergency protocol (including 911 if imminent risk), because safety is priority and the trap is placing them on hold or transferring without warm handoff.
Question 822

A provider asks you to “document that the patient was counseled on medication risks” even though you were not present; what is the best response?

Decline and document only what you personally observed/performed, because falsifying records is illegal/unethical and the trap is thinking you’re helping the provider with “standard wording.”
Question 823

A patient asks on the phone, “Does my plan cover this visit?” and you are unsure; what should you do?

Explain you can’t guarantee coverage, then follow the clinic process to verify eligibility/benefits or refer to billing, because coverage depends on the payer and the trap is assuring the patient it’s “definitely covered.”
Question 824

You chart “patient denies chest pain” but later realize they actually said they had intermittent pressure; what should you do?

Enter an addendum/correction noting the accurate statement and that the prior entry was incorrect, then alert the provider if it affects care, because clinical accuracy impacts safety and the trap is changing the original note without disclosure.
Question 825

A patient leaves a voicemail requesting a refill and gives a different pharmacy than listed; what should the CMA do next?

Confirm the patient’s identity and pharmacy details, then route the request to the provider for authorization, because pharmacy changes can cause errors and the trap is sending it to the “usual” pharmacy without verifying.
Question 826

At check-out, a patient asks you to explain a new diagnosis and whether it means they “have cancer”; what is the best CMA response?

Acknowledge the concern and refer the question to the provider while offering to help schedule a call/visit, because interpreting diagnoses is outside CMA scope and the trap is trying to reassure with speculation.
Question 827

You are entering vitals and notice another patient’s chart is open in the EHR; what should you do first?

Stop and open the correct patient chart using two identifiers before documenting anything, because wrong-chart documentation is a major safety/legal risk and the trap is “fixing it later” after entering data.
Question 828

A patient wants a copy of their full medical record today and becomes upset when told it may take time; what is the CMA’s best response?

Explain the release-of-information process, verify identity, and route the request to the designated records/ROI workflow because records must be released securely and completely, and the trap is handing out printouts without authorization or identity verification.
Question 829

While rooming a patient, the CMA notices the provider already signed a note that incorrectly states the patient denied a penicillin allergy, but the intake form and the patient both report hives with penicillin—what should the CMA do NEXT?

Notify the provider immediately and document the patient-reported allergy in the designated allergy section with date/time and source; do not alter the provider’s signed note because the priority is preventing a medication error and maintaining a legal, traceable record (common trap: “fixing” the note yourself or waiting until checkout).
Question 830

A patient asks you at the front desk, “Did my neighbor get treated here for an STI?” and says they’re “just worried about them”; what should the CMA do next?

Politely refuse to confirm or deny and redirect the patient to speak with their neighbor directly because any disclosure of a patient’s presence or condition is a HIPAA violation, and the common trap is thinking good intentions make it permissible.
Question 831

A provider tells you, “Go ahead and document that the patient denies chest pain,” but the patient never said that; what should the CMA do?

Document only what you personally observed/what the patient actually stated and clarify with the provider because falsifying documentation is illegal, and the trap is charting “for convenience” to match the provider’s note.
Question 832

A parent arrives with a 16-year-old for a pregnancy test and demands the result at checkout; what should the CMA do first?

Verify the clinic’s consent/privacy rules for minors and refer the request to the provider/authorized staff because disclosure may be restricted even to parents, and the trap is assuming a parent automatically has access.
Question 833

A patient asks you to “change the diagnosis code” so their insurance will cover a test; what should the CMA do?

Refuse and route the request to billing/provider because altering codes to obtain payment is fraud, and the trap is believing you’re “helping the patient” by making it billable.
Question 834

During a phone call, a person says, “I’m her husband—tell me her lab results,” and can state her DOB but isn’t listed as an authorized contact; what should the CMA do?

Decline to share results and ask the patient to call or submit authorization because verification requires permitted access, not just identifiers, and the trap is treating DOB/name as sufficient permission.
Question 835

You accidentally email an after-visit summary to the wrong patient address; what should the CMA do next?

Immediately notify the privacy officer/supervisor and follow the breach-reporting procedure because timely reporting limits harm and is required, and the trap is trying to “fix it quietly” by sending a second email.
Question 836

A patient at check-in says, “My spouse can’t make it—can you tell them my lab results over the phone later?” and the chart shows no release-of-information authorization on file; what should the CMA do next?

Tell the patient you can’t disclose results to the spouse without the patient’s written authorization (or the patient speaking directly on the call) and offer to have the patient complete an ROI form now, because HIPAA requires verifying permission and a common error is sharing information based on verbal requests or family status.
Question 837

A patient’s insurance card is expired but they insist you “bill it anyway” and see them now; what should the CMA do next?

Inform the patient the coverage can’t be verified and follow the clinic policy for self-pay/deposit while offering to reschedule if needed because billing unverified insurance increases denials, and the trap is promising coverage without verification.
Question 838

You discover a coworker leaving printed schedules with patient names and reasons for visit on an open counter; what is the CMA’s best immediate action?

Secure the documents (turn them face down/remove to a private area) and report the privacy risk to a supervisor because visible PHI is an avoidable disclosure, and the trap is ignoring it because “staff are the only ones here.”
Question 839

In the EHR you notice a progress note has the wrong date of service auto-populated (yesterday instead of today); what should the CMA do?

Correct it using the EHR’s amendment process before signing/finalizing because date/time are legal elements of the record, and the trap is signing anyway assuming “it doesn’t matter.”
Question 840

A patient signs a consent form but says, “I don’t really understand what this procedure is”; what should the CMA do first?

Pause the process and notify the provider to re-explain and confirm understanding because valid informed consent requires comprehension, and the trap is proceeding because a signature is already on the paper.
Question 841

A patient checks in under a similar name to another patient (same last name, close DOB) and the front desk starts creating a new chart; what should the CMA do first?

Stop and verify identity using two identifiers and search for existing records before creating a new chart because duplicate charts create medication/allergy errors, and the trap is assuming “new patient” based on a quick search.
Question 842

During checkout, a patient asks you to explain a new medication’s side effects in detail and whether it will interact with their supplements; what should the CMA do best?

Provide general, non-clinical instructions already given (e.g., read the label) and refer the question to the provider or pharmacist because counseling on interactions is outside CMA scope, and the trap is giving specific interaction advice to be reassuring.
Question 843

You need to leave a voicemail for a patient about “abnormal results”; what is the best practice for the message?

Leave minimal information (clinic name/callback number) without sensitive details unless the patient has agreed to detailed messages because voicemail can be accessed by others, and the trap is stating the diagnosis or results to be “helpful.”
Question 844

A patient presents for a procedure and says they never received the pre-procedure instructions that were “sent in the portal”; what should the CMA do best?

Confirm the patient’s preferred contact method, provide the approved instructions, and document the education and patient understanding because missing instructions affects safety and compliance, and the trap is blaming the patient or proceeding without re-education.
Question 845

At check-in, a patient asks to update their address and then requests a printed copy of their most recent lab results to “show my spouse,” but the spouse is not present and the patient’s chart has no release-of-information authorization on file; what should the CMA do next?

Verify the patient’s identity, update the demographic information in the EHR, and provide the labs directly to the patient (not to the spouse) while explaining that results can only be released to others with the patient’s written authorization because HIPAA permits patient access but sharing with third parties without authorization is a common compliance error.
Question 846

While scheduling, a patient asks for a same-day appointment for medication refills and mentions dizziness when standing; what should the CMA do first?

Triage the call by escalating promptly to the clinical team/provider for guidance while scheduling because dizziness could indicate an urgent issue, and the trap is treating it as routine refills and booking the next available slot.
Question 847

A claim is denied because the insurer says “prior authorization required”; what is the CMA’s best next step within scope?

Route the denial to the appropriate authorization/billing workflow and gather required documentation (order, diagnosis, notes) for resubmission because denials are resolved with correct admin follow-up, and the trap is telling the patient the clinic “can’t do anything.”
Question 848

A patient calls asking for their spouse’s lab results and says, “I’m on the HIPAA form somewhere,” while you are at the front desk with other patients nearby; what should the CMA do FIRST?

Move the call to a private setting and verify the caller’s identity and documented authorization in the chart before sharing any information, because assuming relationship or “I’m listed” is a common HIPAA breach trap.
Question 849

A minor arrives with an adult who claims to be an aunt and requests a pregnancy test; the minor looks anxious and says, “Don’t tell my parents”; what should the CMA do NEXT?

Pause testing and involve the provider/office supervisor to confirm who can legally consent based on state law and clinic policy before proceeding, because letting a non-guardian consent (or promising secrecy) is a common consent and disclosure trap.
Question 850

During scheduling, a patient requests an appointment for “chest tightness that started this morning” and insists they only want the next available slot tomorrow; what should the CMA do FIRST?

Advise the patient to seek immediate emergency care/activate EMS per clinic protocol and notify the clinical team, because delaying care by booking routinely is a high-risk triage trap even in an administrative call.
Question 851

A patient is scheduled for a referral MRI and says they are “a little claustrophobic” and want you to “just tell the imaging place to give something to relax”; what is the CMA’s best next action?

Tell the patient you cannot request or promise sedatives and notify the provider/referral coordinator for proper evaluation/instructions, because medication decisions are outside CMA scope and the trap is informally arranging sedation.
Question 852

During check-in, a patient asks for a printed copy of yesterday’s visit note and says, “Don’t tell my spouse,” but the spouse is listed in the chart as an emergency contact; what should the CMA do next?

Verify the patient’s identity and provide the record only to the patient after confirming there is no signed authorization on file for the spouse, because emergency contact status does not grant HIPAA access and the common mistake is disclosing information to family without explicit permission.
Question 853

A patient at check-in asks to “see my spouse’s lab results too since we share insurance,” and the spouse is not present; what should the CMA do next?

Decline to release any information and explain HIPAA requires the spouse’s written authorization or verified legal access before disclosure, then offer to have the spouse complete a release; sharing insurance is a common trap that does not grant access to records.
Question 854

During diabetes teaching, a patient nods and says “yeah, I get it” while scrolling their phone; what should the CMA do first to confirm understanding before the patient leaves?

Ask the patient to teach-back the key steps in their own words (e.g., when to check glucose and what to do for low readings) because agreement isn’t comprehension, and the trap is ending education after a yes/no response.
Question 855

A patient is checking out and asks you to “just change the diagnosis code” so their insurance will cover a test that was denied; what is the BEST response?

Explain you cannot change codes to obtain payment and route the concern to billing/provider for review of documentation and medical necessity, because altering codes without support is fraud and outside the CMA’s role.
Question 856

The provider tells you verbally to “refill the patient’s blood pressure meds for 6 months” and asks you to send it to the pharmacy; what should the CMA do?

Do not authorize the refill; document the request and route it through the provider’s approved prescribing workflow (e.g., provider signs/e-prescribes) because CMAs cannot prescribe and “sending it in” is a scope-of-practice trap.
Question 857

A patient receives abnormal Pap results by portal message and calls upset saying, “So I have cancer?”; what should the CMA do first?

Acknowledge feelings and schedule the provider follow-up while avoiding interpretation of results, because CMAs shouldn’t diagnose and the trap is reassuring with clinical conclusions instead of arranging timely follow-up.
Question 858

A patient asks you to send their records to a new specialist and says, “Just fax everything to this number my friend gave me”; what should the CMA do first?

Verify the receiving office identity and obtain proper written authorization/release before sending, because misdirected records are a HIPAA breach and the trap is faxing based on unverified contact info.
Question 859

At checkout, a patient with hypertension says, “I can’t afford these meds so I’m taking them every other day”; what should the CMA do next?

Use nonjudgmental teach-back to assess what they’re actually taking and route the concern to the provider/team for cost alternatives or assistance resources, because dose-changing is unsafe and the trap is simply documenting without escalation.
Question 860

A patient with a positive fecal immunochemical test says they feel fine and asks to “wait a few months” before colonoscopy; what is the CMA’s best next action?

Reinforce the need for timely diagnostic follow-up and coordinate the referral/scheduling process, because feeling well doesn’t rule out serious disease and the trap is treating screening follow-up as optional.
Question 861

A parent declines the second dose of a vaccine series today saying, “We missed the last appointment so we’ll just restart later”; what is the CMA’s best response?

Explain the series typically does not need restarting and arrange the next due date using the clinic recall system, because restarting delays protection and the trap is accepting the parent’s plan without scheduling follow-up.
Question 862

You discover a missed appointment for a patient with uncontrolled diabetes and no future visits scheduled; what should the CMA do next to reduce error risk?

Initiate the clinic’s recall/outreach process (call/text/letter per protocol) and document the attempt, because missed follow-up is a common safety failure and the trap is assuming the patient will reschedule on their own.
Question 863

After teaching a patient how to use a home blood pressure cuff, they say, “I’ll check it whenever I feel stressed”; what is the CMA’s best next action?

Clarify the provider’s monitoring schedule and use teach-back to confirm when/how often to measure and how to record readings, because inconsistent timing skews results and the trap is leaving instructions vague.
Question 864

A patient with a new inhaler prescription demonstrates use by spraying into the air and then inhaling; what should the CMA do next?

Re-teach correct technique and have the patient re-demonstrate (teach-back) because demonstration verifies skill, and the trap is giving verbal instructions only and assuming they’ll do it correctly at home.
Question 865

A patient returns from a referral visit saying the specialist “never got the referral” and they were turned away; what is the CMA’s best next step?

Verify referral transmission status and resend/confirm receipt with the specialist while documenting and offering to reschedule, because coordination prevents repeat delays and the trap is blaming the patient or telling them to “call again.”
Question 866

After giving lifestyle counseling, you document “patient understands” and close the chart; what is the best documentation practice to prevent errors?

Document specific teach-back results (what the patient stated they will do and any barriers) because vague phrases don’t prove comprehension, and the trap is using canned statements that won’t support continuity of care.
Question 867

A patient calls and asks for lab results; you see the result is flagged abnormal but not yet reviewed by the provider; what should the CMA do?

Inform the patient the provider must review and you will route it for prompt review/callback per protocol, because releasing unreviewed abnormal results risks misinterpretation and the trap is reading values over the phone.
Question 868

At check-in, a patient asks, “Can you tell my employer what my diagnosis is so I can get leave?”; what is the CMA’s best response?

Explain you can provide documentation only with the patient’s authorization and typically as a provider-completed form/letter, because disclosing diagnoses without consent violates privacy and the trap is oversharing to “help.”
Question 869

A patient asks for help completing an insurance prior authorization form and wants you to “write why this medication is medically necessary”; what is the CMA’s best action?

Collect required nonclinical information and route the clinical justification sections to the provider, because medical necessity statements are provider-level and the trap is writing clinical rationale outside scope.
Question 870

A patient who speaks limited English is handed printed prep instructions for a referral procedure and says “okay” repeatedly; what should the CMA do before discharge?

Arrange a qualified interpreter and use teach-back to confirm key prep steps, because language barriers drive prep failures and the trap is relying on nodding or family members instead of proper interpretation.
Question 871

While rooming, a patient says they stopped an antibiotic early because they “felt better”; what should the CMA do next?

Provide education using teach-back on completing prescribed courses and notify the provider if symptoms persist or complications are possible, because early stopping can cause relapse/resistance and the trap is simply advising “finish it” without verifying understanding.
Question 872

Advise them to stop taking any new supplement until they speak with the provider and escalate the message urgently for clinical guidance, because interactions can increase bleeding risk and the trap is treating supplements as harmless.
Question 873

Ask the patient to repeat back the exact dosing schedule in their own words and clarify using the labeled instructions, because teach-back verifies understanding and the trap is accepting “yes” responses that hide confusion.
Question 874

Inform the provider and reschedule or follow the provider’s direction after disclosure, because accuracy depends on proper preparation and the trap is collecting a compromised specimen to “save time.”
Question 875

Question 876

Schedule the next available appointment and document the outreach and patient response, because recall and documentation reduce gaps in care and the trap is ending the call without a confirmed plan.
Question 877

Explore barriers and connect them with scheduling options and community resources (e.g., extended hours, transportation assistance) while initiating referral/appointment support, because practical barriers drive nonadherence and the trap is only repeating “it’s important.”
Question 878

Use plain language and ask for teach-back on the key next step (what test, when, and how to prepare), because comprehension—not handouts—drives follow-through and the trap is assuming written materials are enough.
Question 879

Question 880

Question 883

Question 892

After educating a patient on home blood pressure logging, they bring back a sheet with random times and missing units and say, “You know what I mean”; what should the CMA do next?

Re-teach a simple logging method (same time daily, record systolic/diastolic and heart rate) and use teach-back to confirm, because consistent data supports provider decisions and the trap is accepting unusable logs as “close enough.”
Question 893

A parent asks how to measure 5 mL of liquid medication at home and says they usually use a kitchen teaspoon; what should the CMA do next?

Instruct to use an oral syringe or dosing cup marked in mL and confirm with teach-back, because household spoons are inaccurate and the trap is giving a volume-to-teaspoon conversion without proper measuring tools.
Question 894

A patient with newly prescribed metformin says, “I’ll just double it on days I eat sweets,” and asks you to note it in the chart—what should the CMA do first?

Use teach-back to restate the prescribed dosing and instruct the patient not to self-adjust, then alert the provider because “doubling” is a medication-safety risk; the trap is passively documenting unsafe plans without correcting or escalating.
Question 895

While checking in a patient, another patient in the waiting room asks loudly, “Why is he here today?” and points to the schedule screen you’re viewing; what should the CMA do first?

Turn the screen away/lock it and redirect the question without confirming any information, because protecting PHI is immediate priority and the trap is giving even small hints that confirm a visit reason.
Question 896

A patient referred for a sleep study says insurance denied it and asks you to “fix it today”; what is the CMA’s best next step?

Verify the denial reason and route it to the appropriate staff/provider for prior authorization or alternate documentation, because authorization is a common breakdown point and the trap is telling the patient to “call insurance” without initiating the clinic workflow.
Question 897

A patient with a new referral asks, “Can you tell me if this specialist is covered?” and you notice their insurance on file is from last year—what should the CMA do next?

Verify and update insurance information before sending the referral and then coordinate with the payer/referral requirements; the trap is sending referrals with outdated coverage, causing denials and delayed care.
Question 898

A patient calls asking, “What did my biopsy show?” and says they are the patient’s spouse—what is the CMA’s best next step?

Verify authorization to release information (or speak only with the patient) and route clinical result discussion to the provider per clinic process; the trap is assuming relationship equals permission and disclosing protected information.
Question 899

A teen patient asks for STI testing results at the front desk while their parent stands beside them—what should the CMA do first?

Move to a private area and verify what information can be disclosed per privacy rules and consent before discussing results; the trap is answering in a public space, creating a HIPAA breach.
Question 900

A patient with a positive FIT test result missed their GI referral appointment and says, “If it was serious, someone would have called me again”—what should the CMA do next?

Initiate the clinic’s recall process by contacting the patient, reinforcing urgency, and coordinating rescheduling with the referral site while documenting outreach; the trap is assuming “no-show” equals refusal and letting abnormal results lapse.
