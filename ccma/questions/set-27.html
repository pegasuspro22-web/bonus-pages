Questions 2601–2700

Question 2601

A patient asks whether it’s okay to take two different OTC cold medicines along with their prescribed medication, but you’re unsure about interactions; what is the CMA’s best action?

Do not recommend a regimen; collect the exact product names/ingredients and route to the provider/pharmacist for guidance, because combination products often duplicate ingredients and the trap is giving advice that could cause overdose.
Question 2602

A prescription label the patient brings in reads “metoprolol 25 mg PO BID,” and the patient says they take it “whenever I feel stressed”; what should the CMA do?

Teach that BID means twice daily as directed and alert the provider about nonadherence, because the trap is assuming the patient understands abbreviations or ignoring unsafe self-directed dosing.
Question 2603

A patient taking an antibiotic says they developed hives and lip swelling after the first dose; what should the CMA do first?

Treat it as a possible severe allergic reaction by directing immediate emergency care per clinic protocol and alerting the provider, because angioedema can progress quickly and the trap is advising antihistamines and “watching it.”
Question 2604

A patient says, “I stopped my steroid pill yesterday because I felt better,” after several weeks of use; what should the CMA do next within scope?

Advise the patient to contact the prescribing provider before stopping and document/route the message, because abrupt steroid discontinuation can be harmful and the trap is giving taper instructions (which is outside CMA scope).
Question 2605

During intake, a patient points to the right lower abdomen and says it hurts; which body quadrant should the CMA document?

Document right lower quadrant (RLQ), because accurate anatomical terminology prevents the trap of vague notes like “lower stomach” that mislead clinical decision-making.
Question 2606

A medication order reads “0.5 mL” but the syringe is marked in “cc”; what should the CMA do to ensure correct dosing?

Treat 1 mL as equivalent to 1 cc and measure 0.5 mL accurately, because the trap is thinking they’re different units and misdosing.
Question 2607

A patient with asthma asks why they need “a bronchodilator” before exercise; what is the best CMA-level explanation?

Explain it relaxes airway smooth muscle to open the bronchi and improve airflow, because the trap is promising it “cures” asthma or giving provider-level counseling.
Question 2608

A patient asks where their “trachea” is during a neck exam; what is the most accurate brief explanation within CMA scope?

Explain it’s the windpipe in the midline of the neck that carries air to the lungs, because the trap is using imprecise terms (e.g., “throat”) that confuse airway vs esophagus.
Question 2609

A patient reports burning with urination and urinary frequency; which system should the CMA recognize as most likely involved when selecting intake questions?

Focus on the urinary system (genitourinary) and ask about fever/flank pain, because the trap is treating it as a gastrointestinal complaint and missing urinary red flags.
Question 2610

The order says “IM injection in the deltoid,” and a new CMA suggests injecting into the antecubital fossa because it’s “easy to find”; what should you do?

Stop and select the correct anatomical site (deltoid muscle of upper arm) per order, because the trap is confusing venipuncture landmarks with intramuscular injection sites.
Question 2611

A patient says they have “syncope” episodes, but when you ask they describe feeling lightheaded without passing out; what is the best CMA follow-up?

Clarify whether there was true loss of consciousness and document the patient’s description, because the trap is accepting medical jargon at face value and misclassifying near-syncope.
Question 2612

A provider asks the CMA to “chart the patient is tachypneic”; the respirations you counted are 18 and unlabored—what should the CMA do?

Document the objective respiration rate/effort you observed and clarify with the provider if needed, because the trap is charting a term that contradicts measured data.
Question 2613

A provider gives a verbal instruction to “start an ACE,” and the patient asks you which medication that is; what should the CMA do?

Tell the patient you will have the provider discuss the specific medication and indication, because the trap is naming or recommending a drug class, which is outside CMA prescribing/counseling authority.
Question 2614

A parent says their teen is taking “one tablet daily” of a new medication but doesn’t know the strength, and the bottle isn’t available; what is the best next step for the CMA?

Obtain the exact name and strength by calling the pharmacy with consent or having the parent bring the bottle, because the trap is documenting incomplete medication details that can lead to dosing or interaction errors.
Question 2615

A caregiver says a child’s acetaminophen is “160 mg/5 mL” and asks how many mL equals 320 mg; what is the correct calculation for the CMA to provide as labeled dosing support?

Give 10 mL (because doubling 160 mg to 320 mg doubles 5 mL to 10 mL), and the trap is mixing mg and mL without using the concentration.
Question 2616

An order states “apply ophthalmic drops,” but the patient thinks it goes in the ear because it’s “the same kind of drop”; what should the CMA do?

Clarify that ophthalmic means eye and demonstrate correct eye-drop technique if instructed, because the trap is confusing ophthalmic vs otic and causing harm.
Question 2617

A patient brings two look-alike insulin pens and says, “I use the blue one at night and the other with meals, but I’m not sure which is which”; what should the CMA do first?

Stop and have the patient identify each pen by the pharmacy label/name (and notify provider/pharmacist if unclear), because the trap is relying on color/appearance, which changes and causes serious errors.
Question 2618

A patient asks if “SL nitroglycerin” should be swallowed like other pills; what is the best CMA response?

Instruct that SL means place under the tongue to dissolve and notify the provider if the patient has been swallowing it, because the trap is treating route abbreviations as optional and reducing effectiveness.
Question 2619

During medication reconciliation, a patient lists “Coumadin” but the EHR lists “warfarin”; what should the CMA do?

Verify they are the same medication (brand vs generic) and document consistently while confirming dose/strength, because the trap is recording them as two separate anticoagulants.
Question 2620

A patient reports “melena” but then says their stool was black after taking bismuth; what should the CMA do with this information?

Document the description and medication use and promptly notify the provider for evaluation, because the trap is dismissing black stools as “just meds” when GI bleeding must be considered.
Question 2621

A provider writes “D/C lisinopril today” and the patient asks if that means “discharge from the clinic”; what should the CMA clarify and do next?

Clarify that “D/C” here means discontinue the medication and notify the provider if the patient is confused, because abbreviation meaning depends on context; the trap is assuming “discharge” and giving incorrect instructions.
Question 2622

During rooming, the provider’s note says “c/o CP x2 hrs,” and the patient points to the center of the chest and looks pale; what should the CMA do first before continuing intake questions?

Stop the routine intake and alert the provider/activate the clinic’s emergency response immediately because “CP” can indicate acute cardiac symptoms; the trap is treating it as a minor complaint abbreviation and delaying escalation.
Question 2623

A teen tells the CMA, “I’ve had nausea and dizziness since starting my new med,” and the mother says, “She seems fine”; how should the CMA document signs vs symptoms?

Document nausea and dizziness as symptoms (patient-reported) and note any objective findings you observe (e.g., unsteady gait) as signs; the trap is charting caregiver opinions as objective “signs.”
Question 2624

During intake, a patient describes “burning when I pee and going all the time”; which system is most directly involved for accurate documentation and routing?

The urinary system because dysuria and frequency commonly reflect urinary tract involvement; the trap is documenting it as a “GI issue” due to abdominal discomfort wording.
Question 2625

While updating the medication list, a prescription label reads “metoprolol 25 mg bid,” and the patient says they take it “whenever I remember”; what is the CMA’s best response?

Confirm that “bid” means twice daily and educate on taking it consistently as directed, then notify the provider of nonadherence; the trap is simply recording what the patient does without addressing the safety risk.
Question 2626

A patient with diabetes says they have “pins-and-needles” in both feet; which system connection should the CMA recognize when flagging the chart?

Nervous system involvement (peripheral neuropathy pattern) should be flagged because it can affect sensation and injury risk; the trap is treating it as a minor skin complaint and not connecting it to neuro symptoms.
Question 2627

A patient with “hx of COPD” reports increased wheezing today; which body system should the CMA prioritize when reporting the concern to the provider?

Prioritize the respiratory system because COPD affects airflow and worsening wheeze may indicate breathing compromise; the trap is focusing on unrelated systems and delaying respiratory assessment/escalation.
Question 2628

An order says “apply medication topically to the affected area,” but the patient asks if they should swallow it since it’s “medicine”; what should the CMA do?

Explain that “topical” means applied to the skin and review the label/site of application, because route errors are common; the trap is assuming the patient understands and not preventing a wrong-route exposure.
Question 2629

A patient states, “I’m spotting between periods and feel unusually tired,” and the chart notes heavy menses last month; what system should the CMA consider when prioritizing follow-up questions?

The reproductive/hematologic connection should be considered (abnormal uterine bleeding with possible anemia symptoms like fatigue); the trap is charting fatigue alone without linking it to potential blood loss.
Question 2630

A patient asks whether they can split a tablet to “make it last longer,” and the label indicates “extended-release”; what should the CMA do?

Instruct the patient not to split/crush extended-release tablets and to consult the provider/pharmacy for alternatives, because altering ER forms can cause dose dumping; the trap is treating all tablets as safely splittable.
Question 2631

The provider orders “ibuprofen 600 mg” and the patient says they also take an OTC “pain reliever” for arthritis but can’t recall the name; what is the CMA’s best next step?

Ask the patient to bring in the OTC bottle or call with the exact name/strength before starting additional NSAIDs, because duplication increases GI/renal risk; the trap is accepting “pain reliever” as sufficient.
Question 2632

A prescription is written for “1 tablet qd,” but the patient asks if taking it “every other day” is close enough; what should the CMA say?

Reinforce that “qd” means every day as prescribed and advise the patient to discuss any schedule changes with the provider, because altering frequency can reduce effectiveness; the trap is giving approval for a self-adjusted regimen.
Question 2633

A patient reports right upper quadrant abdominal pain after greasy meals and nausea; which organ system is most likely implicated for focused history questions?

The digestive/hepatobiliary system (gallbladder/liver area) because the symptom pattern aligns with biliary issues; the trap is focusing only on “stomach flu” and missing the RUQ clue.
Question 2634

A patient says, “I’m allergic to penicillin—my throat swelled up,” and the provider plans to prescribe an antibiotic; what should the CMA do within scope?

Document the reaction type clearly and immediately flag/notify the provider because throat swelling suggests anaphylaxis risk; the trap is recording “PCN allergy” without details and missing severity.
Question 2635

While reconciling meds, the patient lists “Tylenol” and “acetaminophen” as two separate items taken daily; what should the CMA do next?

Identify they are the same medication and clarify total daily intake to prevent duplicate dosing, then notify the provider if doses are high; the trap is leaving both on the list and missing overdose risk.
Question 2636

A provider orders “0.5 mL IM” of a vaccine, but the vial concentration is different from what the CMA is used to seeing; what should the CMA do before drawing it up?

Verify the ordered dose (mL) against the specific product’s label and medication record and clarify discrepancies before preparation, because concentration/volume confusion causes dosing errors; the trap is relying on memory of “usual” volumes.
Question 2637

During rooming, a patient says “I’ve had vertigo for 2 days,” but the chart note from the last visit says “dizziness”; what should the CMA do next to document accurately?

Ask the patient to describe what they mean (spinning vs lightheaded) and document the patient’s exact words because “vertigo” is a specific symptom pattern and the trap is copying the prior term without clarification.
Question 2638

A patient reports “nocturia” when reviewing the history form; what focused follow-up should the CMA ask to correctly interpret the term?

Ask how many times they wake at night to urinate and document the frequency because nocturia is nighttime urination frequency and the trap is treating it as daytime “frequency” without timing.
Question 2639

A patient’s medication list shows “mg” on one entry and “mcg” on another for a similar drug name; what should the CMA do before confirming the list as accurate?

Verify the exact drug and strength with the prescription bottle or pharmacy record because mg vs mcg mix-ups can be 1000-fold errors; the trap is assuming they’re interchangeable units.
Question 2640

The provider orders a medication “subcutaneous,” but the patient assumes it goes into muscle since it’s an injection; what should the CMA clarify before administration?

Clarify that subcutaneous injections go into fatty tissue (not muscle) and select the correct site/technique per standard practice, because route/site errors affect absorption and safety; the trap is proceeding with an IM technique.
Question 2641

At the front desk, a patient says, “My spouse can pick up my lab results if you tell them over the phone,” and no release is documented; what should the CMA do?

Do not disclose results and explain that written permission (or documented authorization) is needed before discussing protected information, because HIPAA prohibits sharing without proper authorization; the trap is assuming marital status equals automatic access.
Question 2642

A provider’s order reads “MS 2 mg IM,” and the patient has multiple medication allergies listed; what should the CMA do before preparing the injection?

Hold the medication and clarify the abbreviation with the provider because “MS” can be confused (morphine sulfate vs magnesium sulfate) and the trap is assuming the intended drug from context.
Question 2643

A teen arrives with a parent, and the parent asks to hear all results while the teen answers questions; what is the CMA’s best initial response using correct terminology and boundaries?

Ask the teen privately who they want present and what can be shared because confidentiality/authorization may apply and the trap is automatically disclosing protected information to the parent.
Question 2644

A patient scheduled for a minor in-office procedure asks, “Do I need to sign anything?” and the provider is running behind; what is the CMA’s best action?

Ensure the correct consent form is present and alert the provider to obtain informed consent before the procedure, because the CMA cannot obtain informed consent independently; the trap is having the patient sign without provider discussion.
Question 2645

Recognize this is cardiovascular/renal-related fluid retention and document swelling location and severity because edema is fluid accumulation and the trap is charting it as “weight gain” without describing swelling.
Question 2646

Document the symptom details (onset, location, numbness/tingling) and notify the provider without diagnosing because paresthesia can have many causes and the trap is confirming diabetes or offering medical interpretation.
Question 2647

Question 2648

Document “history of myocardial infarction (patient reports heart attack)” because MI is a common abbreviation that should be clearly expanded and the trap is leaving unclear shorthand that can be misread.
Question 2649

Question 2650
