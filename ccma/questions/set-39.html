Question 3801

A patient leaves a voicemail saying, “Call me back at my work number with my test results,” but the chart notes “do not contact at work”; what should the CMA do best?

Follow the documented communication preference and use an approved alternative (secure portal/home number) or confirm updated permission with the patient, because honoring privacy preferences prevents unintended disclosure and the trap is following the most recent voicemail instruction without reconciling the chart.
Question 3802

A teen arrives for a visit and asks you not to tell their parent what the visit is for; the parent demands details at check-in—what should the CMA do first?

Move the conversation to a private area and follow state/clinic rules by involving the provider/manager before disclosing anything, because discussing details at the front desk is a common privacy violation.
Question 3803

A provider tells you to “just put normal” for a patient’s pain score because the clinic is behind; what should the CMA do?

Document the patient’s actual reported pain score (or reassess and document) and refuse to falsify, because altering clinical data for speed creates legal risk and patient-safety harm.
Question 3804

You document a blood pressure in the EHR and then notice you typed 180/80 instead of 118/80; what is the correct way to fix it?

Enter a corrected entry using the EHR’s amendment/correction function with date/time and reason, because deleting or overwriting without an audit trail is improper documentation.
Question 3805

During checkout, you overhear staff discussing a patient’s STI results in the hallway; what is the most appropriate CMA action?

Redirect the conversation to a private area and remind the team to avoid public disclosures, because “staff-only hallway” talk is a common HIPAA trap.
Question 3806

At check-in, two patients with the same first and last name arrive within minutes; what should the CMA do to prevent documentation errors?

Use at least two identifiers (e.g., full name and date of birth) and confirm the correct chart before documenting or scanning, because relying on name alone is a common wrong-chart error.
Question 3807

A patient’s allergy list in the EHR is blank, but they tell you they had an anaphylactic reaction to penicillin; what should the CMA do next?

Enter the allergy with reaction details per clinic process and alert the provider before any medications are ordered, because leaving it as “no known allergies” is a high-risk documentation trap.
Question 3808

A patient asks you to print their full chart for “personal reasons” and wants it immediately; what should you do first?

Verify identity and follow the facility’s release-of-information process (often through records/ROI with documented request), because handing over a chart without proper workflow can release wrong or excessive information.
Question 3809

You receive a faxed record request from an attorney with no patient signature attached; what should the CMA do next?

Route it for verification and obtain a valid signed authorization before releasing records, because legal letterhead alone is not permission to disclose PHI.
Question 3810

A provider documents late and asks you to enter the provider’s physical exam findings for them since you were in the room; what is the best response?

Do not document findings you didn’t personally perform/observe as your own; instead, document only what you did and notify the provider to complete their note, because “helping” can become falsification and misattribution.
Question 3811

At check-in, a patient becomes loud and demands you confirm whether their spouse had “that STD test” at your clinic last week; the spouse is not present and there is no authorization on file—what should the CMA do first?

Calmly state you cannot confirm or deny any patient’s care without written permission, redirect to discuss only the requesting patient’s own visit, and offer the spouse the proper release process; this protects HIPAA—common trap is “just confirming they were seen,” which is still a disclosure.
Question 3812

While documenting a phone call, the patient uses profanity and makes threats; what is the best documentation approach?

Document objective facts and direct quotes as needed without added opinions, because editorializing (“patient is crazy”) is a common charting error that harms legal defensibility.
Question 3813

A patient leaves a voicemail with full name, date of birth, and detailed symptoms and asks for a callback at work; what is the safest callback approach?

Call back using the number provided but verify identity first and avoid leaving detailed PHI on voicemail unless the patient’s permission/preferences are documented, because detailed messages are a common privacy breach.
Question 3814

A patient arrives for a procedure and says they never signed the consent form; the provider is running behind and asks you to “just have them sign”; what should the CMA do?

Ensure the patient receives the provider’s explanation and has questions answered before obtaining the signature (and notify the provider if consent wasn’t done), because a signature without informed discussion is not valid consent.
Question 3815

A patient says their insurer “will cover it” but the EHR shows the policy expired last month; what should the CMA do next?

Verify and update insurance eligibility/coverage before the visit (or route to billing/registration) because assuming coverage leads to denials and unexpected patient balances.
Question 3816

A patient calls asking for their spouse’s medication list “for an emergency,” and the caller can correctly state the spouse’s DOB and address; what is the CMA’s best next step?

Verify authorization (release/permission on file) and, if not present, decline and offer to speak directly with the patient or have the patient complete a release because identity knowledge alone is not legal consent; the trap is assuming correct identifiers equal permission.
Question 3817

A patient calls asking, “What does my diagnosis code mean?” and wants medical interpretation; what should the CMA do?

Provide non-clinical clarification only (e.g., that codes are for billing) and route clinical meaning/questions to the provider, because interpreting diagnoses is outside CMA scope.
Question 3818

While rooming a patient, you overhear a coworker discussing a celebrity patient’s lab results in the hallway where other patients can hear; what should you do first?

Stop the conversation and move it to a private area (or ask them to lower their voice and step away) because HIPAA requires minimizing incidental disclosure; the trap is ignoring it because “they didn’t say the name” when details can still identify the patient.
Question 3819

The provider documents a preventive visit plus an in-depth evaluation for new chest discomfort; the patient expects “no copay”; what should the CMA say?

Explain that insurance may process additional problem-focused services separately and billing will follow payer rules, because promising “free” care is a common front-desk communication trap.
Question 3820

A claim was denied for “incorrect subscriber ID,” and the patient is upset; what is the best CMA action?

Question 3821

Question 3822

Question 3824

Question 3829

Question 3831

Question 3832

Question 3896

A patient leaves a voicemail asking to be called back with test results, and the voicemail greeting is the patient’s name plus a shared family number; what should the CMA do?

Return the call but avoid leaving detailed results on voicemail unless the patient has documented consent for voicemail disclosure because message-content oversharing is a common HIPAA violation.
Question 3897

You receive a faxed request from a specialist for the patient’s last visit note, but there is no release form attached; what should the CMA do next?

Verify there is a valid authorization or appropriate treatment-related disclosure basis before sending records because “they’re a doctor” is not automatic permission to release.
Question 3898

The front desk double-books a complex procedure in a 15-minute slot and the patient is already in the lobby; what should the CMA do first?

Notify the provider/lead immediately to reprioritize and adjust expectations because silently trying to “make it fit” often leads to rushed care and documentation errors.
Question 3899

A parent at the desk demands details about their 19-year-old child’s visit and says, “I’m paying for it”; what should the CMA do?

Do not disclose without the adult patient’s written permission and offer to have the patient contact the parent directly because payment/relationship does not override adult patient privacy rights.
Question 3900

While rooming a patient, you notice the provider’s note in the EHR auto-populated “denies allergies,” but the patient states they have an anaphylactic penicillin allergy; what should the CMA do FIRST?

Update the allergy field per clinic protocol and immediately alert the provider before any orders are carried out because allergy reconciliation is a safety-critical documentation item; the trap is leaving it for the provider later or burying it only in free-text.
