Question 101

During a fasting lipid panel draw, you collect a light blue citrate tube and then a serum separator tube (gold) from the same venipuncture; the citrate tube is only half full and you already gently inverted it—what should the CMA do next to prevent an inaccurate result?

Stop and redraw the citrate tube to the proper fill line before sending specimens, because underfilling alters the blood-to-anticoagulant ratio and can invalidate coagulation testing; a common mistake is “topping off” from another tube or sending it anyway since it looks mixed.
Question 102

A clinic glucose meter displays “QC overdue” when you attempt a fingerstick glucose; the patient is waiting and says they feel fine; what should the CMA do first?

Perform the required quality control (or obtain a compliant meter) before testing, because regulatory compliance and accuracy come before convenience; the trap is bypassing QC to “save time.”
Question 103

You are running a CLIA-waived rapid strep test and the external control is out of range; what should the CMA do next?

Do not report patient results, troubleshoot/repeat QC per manufacturer instructions and notify the supervisor if unresolved, because failed QC invalidates testing; the trap is reporting “because the patient line looks positive.”
Question 104

You obtain a 12-lead ECG on a patient with new chest pressure and the machine interpretation flags “acute MI suspected”; what should the CMA do next?

Immediately notify the provider per urgent protocol and keep the patient monitored/at rest, because CMAs do not interpret or diagnose but must escalate critical findings; the trap is reassuring the patient or filing the ECG to review later.
Question 105

A fasting lipid panel specimen is drawn correctly, but you realize the label was placed on the counter and not affixed in front of the patient; what is the CMA’s BEST next step?

Re-verify patient identity with two identifiers and label the tube immediately in the patient’s presence (or follow recollection protocol if identity cannot be assured) because unlabeled/mislabeled specimens are never “guessed”—the trap is labeling later from memory.
Question 106

For a glucose tolerance test, a patient arrives late and asks to “just draw the next tube now and mark the time later”; what should the CMA do FIRST?

Clarify the testing schedule and exact collection times before drawing because timed specimens must match strict intervals for valid interpretation—the trap is drawing without confirming timing and producing unusable results.
Question 107

You attempt a venipuncture and a rapidly expanding swelling appears at the site while blood flow stops; what should the CMA do NEXT?

Release the tourniquet, withdraw the needle, and apply firm pressure with a cold compress as appropriate because this suggests a hematoma/infiltration—the trap is “repositioning the needle” which worsens tissue bleeding.
Question 108

After drawing a potassium level, you see the specimen is hemolyzed (pink/red serum) in the tube; the provider is waiting for results; what should the CMA do?

Notify the lab/provider and recollect per protocol because hemolysis can falsely elevate potassium and other analytes—the trap is sending it anyway “to be fast,” risking harmful clinical decisions.
Question 109

During a fasting lipid panel draw, you collect a light blue tube first, then a serum separator (gold) and lavender; after labeling, you realize the blue tube was underfilled and you have not yet removed the needle—what should the CMA do next?

Release the tourniquet and fill a new light blue tube to the required fill line using the same venipuncture (then mix gently and relabel) because citrate tubes require a precise blood-to-additive ratio, and topping off the underfilled tube with blood from another tube or accepting it as-is is a common error that can invalidate coagulation results.
Question 110

Immediately after blood is drawn, the patient becomes pale and says they feel faint while seated on the exam table; what should the CMA do FIRST?

Assist the patient to a safe position (supine or head lowered) and stay with them while assessing responsiveness because preventing a fall is the priority—the trap is leaving to “get juice” before securing safety.
Question 111

A patient needs a PT/INR and the light blue tube fills only halfway; the patient asks if you can “top it off” from another tube after you remove the needle; what is the BEST action?

Redraw the specimen in a new light blue tube filled to the correct line because coag tests require a precise 9:1 blood-to-anticoagulant ratio—mixing from other tubes is a trap that produces inaccurate clotting times.
Question 112

During venipuncture for a CMP and CBC, you draw a light blue tube first because it’s “more sterile,” then realize the error before drawing the lavender; what should the CMA do NEXT?

Stop and redraw using the correct order of draw (blood cultures if ordered, then light blue, then serum, then green, then lavender) because drawing citrate first can be contaminated by additives and falsely prolong coagulation results—a common trap is “just continuing” and sending compromised tubes.
Question 113

During phlebotomy, the patient reports tingling/shooting pain down the forearm; what is the BEST action?

Stop the draw and remove the needle, then apply pressure and report/document because this can indicate nerve irritation/injury—the trap is continuing because “blood is flowing,” which can cause lasting damage.
Question 114

You are about to run a CLIA-waived rapid strep test, but the external controls have not been documented for the new kit lot; what should the CMA do FIRST?

Perform and document quality control per manufacturer instructions before testing patients because results are not reportable without required QC—the trap is running the patient sample “just this once” to save time.
Question 115

During an EKG on a patient with chest pressure, the machine prints “ACUTE MI?” and the patient looks anxious but stable; what should the CMA do NEXT?

Immediately notify the provider and follow the clinic’s urgent response process because machine interpretations are not definitive, but potential ischemia requires prompt escalation—the trap is reassuring the patient or “waiting to chart” before notifying.
Question 116

A patient on anticoagulant therapy has persistent oozing after venipuncture despite 2 minutes of pressure; what should the CMA do NEXT?

Maintain firm direct pressure longer and escalate to the provider per clinic protocol if bleeding does not stop because anticoagulated patients may require extended hemostasis—the trap is applying a quick bandage and letting them leave.
Question 117

You are performing a fingerstick INR POCT and the first drop of blood is small; you consider “milking” the finger hard to get more blood; what should you do instead?

Wipe away the first drop and gently encourage flow without excessive squeezing because milking dilutes the sample with tissue fluid and can alter results—the trap is forceful squeezing to “get enough blood.”
Question 118

During a routine venipuncture for a CMP and CBC, the CMA draws the EDTA (lavender) tube first and then the serum tube; what should the CMA do next to best protect specimen integrity?

Stop and redraw using the correct order of draw (serum tube before EDTA) because drawing EDTA first can carry over additives and falsely alter chemistry results; the common trap is sending it anyway and assuming the lab will “fix it.”
Question 119

You set up a 12-lead EKG and the tracing shows thick, fuzzy baseline “muscle” artifact while the patient is talking and shivering; what should the CMA do FIRST?

Ask the patient to lie still, relax their arms, and warm them if needed before repeating because motion and muscle tension commonly cause artifact—the trap is immediately replacing electrodes without addressing movement.
Question 120

A provider requests a capillary blood glucose; the patient’s hands are cold and cyanotic at the fingertips; what should the CMA do BEFORE puncture?

Warm the hand (and choose an appropriate puncture site) to improve capillary flow and accuracy because poor perfusion can produce unreliable readings—the trap is sticking immediately and accepting a questionable result.
Question 121

At check-in, a patient says they want to pay today and asks you to print and hand them “the whole chart” from the EHR; what should the CMA do NEXT?

Verify the patient’s identity and provide the appropriate authorization/request for release of records for completion and routing (including any required fees/processing) because EHR access is HIPAA-regulated and handing over a full chart on demand risks improper disclosure and missing required documentation.
Question 122

While scanning outside records into the EHR, the CMA sees a lab report for a different patient mixed into the stack; what is the best next action?

Stop scanning, separate the incorrect document, and route it to the correct patient’s chart via the proper process (and notify a supervisor if needed), because misfiled records can cause clinical harm and are a preventable documentation error.
Question 123

A urine pregnancy POCT shows no control line; the medical assistant is tempted to document it as negative because the test line is also blank; what is the BEST action?

Treat it as an invalid test, repeat with a new device (and verify storage/expiration) because a missing control means the test did not run correctly—the trap is calling it negative and misinforming care.
Question 124

While rooming a patient, the CMA notices yesterday’s progress note in the EHR documents the wrong medication dose (metoprolol 50 mg instead of 25 mg) and it has already been signed by the provider; what should the CMA do next?

Notify the provider immediately and follow the EHR’s addendum/correction process rather than editing the signed note, because altering signed documentation can be considered falsification and could lead to medication errors.
Question 125

A patient asks for their rapid flu test result in the waiting room while other patients can hear, and the result has just been entered; what should the CMA do?

Move the conversation to a private area (or lower voice and verify identity) before discussing results because POCT results are PHI and must be protected—the trap is sharing results publicly because “it’s just a rapid test.”
Question 126

A patient asks at checkout, “Can you print my entire chart for my spouse to pick up later?” and the spouse is not listed anywhere in the record; what is the CMA’s best response?

Explain that records can only be released to the patient or an authorized representative and offer the clinic’s release-of-information process, because handing records to an unverified person is a common HIPAA breach.
Question 127

A parent brings a 16-year-old for a sports physical and asks to be present, but the teen says privately they don’t want the parent in the room; what should the CMA do next?

Escort the teen to a private area and inform the provider so the visit can proceed with appropriate confidentiality rules for minors, because automatically allowing the parent access can violate privacy and local minor-consent protections.
Question 128

A patient calls and asks, “What were my HIV test results?” and provides name and date of birth but sounds rushed and is calling from a noisy public place; what should the CMA do?

Use the clinic’s required identity verification steps and, if policy requires, have the patient return the call from a private setting or use the patient portal/provider callback, because giving sensitive results over an inadequately verified phone call is a high-risk HIPAA error.
Question 129

The provider tells the CMA to “just put in the diagnosis code for strep” to speed up billing, but the visit note only documents sore throat and a pending rapid test; what should the CMA do?

Ask the provider to clarify documentation before coding and code only what is supported (e.g., symptom code until confirmed), because assigning an unsupported diagnosis is a common fraud/denial trigger.
Question 130

A patient says, “My employer needs proof I was here—can you write a note that I was diagnosed with pneumonia?”; what should the CMA do?

Provide only attendance information if allowed (date/time of visit) and route any diagnosis/disability statements to the provider, because the trap is disclosing unnecessary PHI and documenting medical conclusions outside CMA scope.
Question 131

A new patient arrives late and wants to keep the appointment, but the next three patients are already checked in and one is scheduled for a procedure requiring a room; what should the CMA do next?

Inform the patient of the delay and check with the scheduler/provider about rescheduling or working them into the schedule based on clinic policy and acuity, because unilaterally “squeezing in” can disrupt care flow and patient safety.
Question 132

A patient asks the CMA to “add” that they denied chest pain because “it might help with insurance,” even though they reported chest pain during intake; what should the CMA do?

Document the patient’s statements accurately and inform the provider of the discrepancy, because changing documentation to influence billing is falsification and can compromise clinical care.
Question 133

At checkout, a patient says their insurance is “inactive again” but insists they were covered yesterday and asks to be seen anyway; what is the CMA’s best action?

Verify eligibility in the system and explain options (self-pay, reschedule, or financial counseling per clinic process) rather than promising coverage, because assuming active coverage commonly leads to claim denials and patient billing disputes.
Question 134

While documenting an injection, the CMA realizes they typed the lot number incorrectly but the note is not yet signed; what is the best action?

Correct the lot number immediately and ensure the updated entry is accurate before finalizing, because the trap is leaving known errors that affect recall tracking and legal documentation.
Question 135

A patient leaves a voicemail saying, “Call me back with my biopsy results,” but the voicemail greeting is a shared family phone line; what should the CMA do next?

Return the call using approved procedures and avoid leaving detailed PHI on voicemail (ask for a call back or use the portal), because leaving results on a shared voicemail is a frequent privacy breach.
Question 136

A coworker asks the CMA to “quickly look up” their neighbor’s appointment time in the EHR because the neighbor “won’t answer texts”; what should the CMA do?

Refuse and remind the coworker that accessing records without a job-related need is prohibited, because “curiosity access” is a HIPAA violation even if no information is shared.
Question 137

A provider asks the CMA to obtain consent for a minor office procedure, but the patient speaks limited English and the teen’s sibling offers to interpret; what should the CMA do?

Request a qualified medical interpreter per clinic process before proceeding with consent, because using family as interpreters can lead to inaccurate consent and legal liability.
Question 138

A parent calls the clinic asking for their 16-year-old’s lab results and says they “need them now for school,” but the chart has no documentation of consent to release information to the parent; what should the CMA do next?

Verify identity and explain you can’t disclose results without documented authorization, then route the request to the provider/office privacy process to confirm legal consent before any release, because giving results to a caller without verified permission is a common HIPAA breach even when the caller is a family member.
Question 139

A patient asks the CMA to email their visit summary to a new personal email address while standing at the front desk; what should the CMA do?

Use the clinic’s secure method (patient portal or approved encrypted process) and verify the patient’s identity and requested destination, because standard email is often not secure and sending PHI to an unverified address is a preventable breach.
Question 140

Document the patient’s stated reaction in the allergy/comment section and alert the provider to reconcile it, because CMAs should not independently delete/alter clinical history and the trap is “cleaning up” allergies without provider review.
Question 141

A patient becomes angry on the phone about a bill and starts yelling; what is the CMA’s best next step to de-escalate while maintaining professionalism?

Use calm, respectful statements, verify identity, summarize the concern, and offer a specific next step (transfer to billing/financial counselor or send an itemized statement), because arguing or discussing PHI before verification escalates conflict and risks privacy.
Question 142

Contact the sending office to obtain full identifiers before scheduling or documenting the referral, because acting on incomplete identifiers risks wrong-patient errors and misfiled records.
Question 143

Verify orders before proceeding by checking the EHR, contacting the ordering provider/clinic, and documenting the communication, because drawing without a valid order is a common workflow and legal mistake.
Question 144

Question 145

Question 151

Question 152

Obtain a signed authorization specifying what can be released and to whom (or verify legal representative status), because HIPAA limits disclosure and the trap is accepting a verbal request as sufficient.
Question 153

Question 195

At checkout, a patient says, “I’m not sure when I’m supposed to come back,” and the provider’s note includes follow-up “in 2–4 weeks”—what should the CMA do?

Clarify the exact timeframe with the provider or documented orders and then schedule accordingly, because choosing a date arbitrarily can cause missed clinical reassessment.
Question 196

A patient needs physical therapy but reports no transportation; they seem embarrassed and say they’ll “figure it out”—what should the CMA do next?

Offer appropriate community resources (transport services, social work/case management referral if available) and document the barrier, because ignoring social barriers often leads to nonadherence and failed referrals.
Question 197

A patient arrives for a follow-up but says they never received their test results from last week and are angry; the results are in the EHR—what should the CMA do first?

Acknowledge the concern and route the results to the provider for communication (or follow the clinic’s results-notification protocol) while documenting the lapse, because independently interpreting results to “smooth things over” is a common scope trap.
Question 198

A patient has not completed a referral after two outreach attempts and says they are “fine now”; the referral was for concerning symptoms documented at the visit—what should the CMA do next?

Document the patient’s decision/barrier, encourage completion, and notify the provider for further direction, because closing the referral without provider awareness can miss worsening conditions.
Question 199

A patient with limited English is being checked out after a visit and is scheduled for a fasting lipid panel next week; they nod “yes” when told “nothing to eat,” but look confused and ask, “Can I drink coffee?”—what should the CMA do BEST before the patient leaves?

Use a qualified medical interpreter and teach-back with simple, written instructions to confirm the patient can restate exactly what “fasting” means (e.g., no food and only water unless the provider said otherwise), because nodding isn’t understanding and unclear prep commonly leads to an invalid test and missed follow-up.
Question 200

A patient’s spouse calls asking for the biopsy results because “the patient is too anxious to hear it”—what should the CMA do first?

Verify authorization to share information (HIPAA release or documented permission) and otherwise speak directly with the patient, because disclosing results to family without permission is a common privacy violation.
