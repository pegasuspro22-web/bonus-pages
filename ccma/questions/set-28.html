Question 2701

During intake, the patient says they feel “lightheaded,” but their skin is pale and clammy and you note a rapid pulse; which findings are symptoms vs signs for documentation?

Document “lightheaded” as a symptom and pallor/diaphoresis/tachycardia as signs because symptoms are patient-reported while signs are objective, and the trap is charting patient quotes as objective findings.
Question 2702

A patient reports “heartburn” after meals and points to burning behind the sternum; which body system is most directly involved for routing the complaint?

The gastrointestinal system (esophagus/stomach) is most directly involved because reflux-related symptoms are GI, and the trap is routing as cardiac pain without assessing characteristics—if symptoms suggest true chest pain, escalate.
Question 2703

A provider’s order reads “DC lisinopril today,” and the patient asks if they should “take it later tonight instead”; what should the CMA do next?

Clarify with the provider and document the clarification because “DC” can be misread as “discharge,” and the trap is giving medication-direction advice without confirming the intended abbreviation.
Question 2704

A provider writes “UA” and the patient is sent to radiology expecting an ultrasound; what should the CMA do to prevent the error?

Confirm the intended test with the provider before scheduling because “UA” often means urinalysis in outpatient clinics, and the trap is scheduling based on patient assumption rather than verifying abbreviations.
Question 2705

The provider documents “hx of HTN” and the patient asks if that means they “have it right now”; how should the CMA respond?

Explain that “Hx” means past history/known condition and does not describe a current reading because abbreviations guide interpretation of the chart, and the trap is equating history with today’s vital signs.
Question 2706

A patient calls asking if “bid” on their antibiotic label means “when I feel like it”; what should the CMA say?

Explain that “BID” means twice daily as directed and encourage following the exact schedule because misunderstanding frequency leads to treatment failure, and the trap is using vague language like “take it regularly.”
Question 2707

A patient with diabetes reports new numbness and tingling in both feet during a routine visit; what system is most likely affected and why does it matter for documenting the symptom?

The nervous system (peripheral neuropathy) is likely affected, and accurate system-based documentation helps the provider evaluate complications; the trap is charting it as “circulation problem” without noting neuro symptoms.
Question 2708

Question 2709

Question 2710

Question 2712

Question 2713

Question 2714

Verify the medication name, concentration, and correct dose/volume using the label and order (and confirm with pharmacist/provider if unclear) because volume errors occur when assuming standard concentrations, a common trap.
Question 2715

Question 2717

Question 2718

Question 2719

Question 2799

You measure an adult’s BP over a sweatshirt because the room is cold and the patient is in a hurry; the reading is 160/96—what should you do next?

Re-measure on a bare arm with correct cuff size after the patient rests, because clothing/cuff error can falsely elevate BP and the trap is reporting a single improper reading as true hypertension.
Question 2800

A patient with new wheezing after an injection says, “My throat feels tight,” and is struggling to speak—what is the CMA’s best immediate action?

Activate emergency response (call 911/alert provider), position for breathing, and prepare to assist with emergency medications per clinic protocol because this suggests anaphylaxis; the trap is having the patient “wait it out” or only documenting the complaint.
