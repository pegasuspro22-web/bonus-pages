Question 2001

A patient’s intake sheet says “no hx of CAD,” but the patient describes a prior “heart stent”; what should the CMA do to resolve this terminology mismatch?

Clarify the history with the patient and update the record for provider review, because CAD terminology may be misunderstood by patients and the trap is leaving conflicting history unaddressed.
Question 2002

A referral order says the patient needs a “STAT” ultrasound, but the patient wants to schedule “sometime next month”; what should the CMA do first?

Notify the ordering provider/team and prioritize scheduling immediately, because “STAT” indicates urgent timing and the trap is treating it like routine outpatient scheduling.
Question 2003

The provider documents “qhs” for a medication and the patient repeats, “So I take it whenever I go to bed, even at 3 a.m.?”; what should the CMA reinforce?

Reinforce that “qhs” means at bedtime as directed and clarify the patient’s routine with the provider if sleep schedule is irregular, because mis-timing doses is a common adherence/safety trap.
Question 2004

A prescription label reads “take 2 tablets BID” and the patient says they’ve been taking 2 tablets total per day; what should the CMA do next?

Clarify the dosing instruction with teach-back and notify the provider if adherence has been incorrect, because BID means twice daily and the trap is patients interpreting it as “daily.”
Question 2005

While rooming a patient, the CMA observes visible hives on the arms but the patient reports “I feel itchy”; which is the sign and which is the symptom?

Hives are a sign and itching is a symptom, because signs are objective findings you can observe while symptoms are subjective experiences reported by the patient.
Question 2006

A patient asks if “OTC” ibuprofen is safe with their prescribed blood thinner; what is the CMA’s best action?

Escalate the question to the provider/pharmacist and advise the patient not to start new meds until they get guidance, because CMAs don’t counsel on drug interactions and NSAIDs can increase bleeding risk.
Question 2007

During intake a patient says, “I’ve had chest pressure since this morning,” and the chart note from last visit lists “c/o CP”; what should the CMA do next with this abbreviation before documenting?

Ask the patient to clarify what they mean by “CP” and document the patient’s exact words, because “CP” can be mistaken for cerebral palsy or chest pain and guessing creates a safety/documentation error.
Question 2008

A provider writes “MS” in the assessment section and the patient asks what it means; what is the CMA’s best response?

Ask the provider to clarify and then relay the clarified term in plain language, because “MS” is ambiguous (e.g., multiple sclerosis vs morphine sulfate) and the trap is interpreting it independently.
Question 2009

During a medication history, a patient says they are “allergic to penicillin” but describes only nausea; what should the CMA do?

Document the reaction exactly as described and flag it for provider review, because side effects are often misreported as allergies and the trap is labeling it as an anaphylactic allergy without details.
Question 2010

While documenting, a CMA considers writing “U” for insulin units to save time; what is the safest documentation practice?

Write out “units” completely, because “U” can be misread as a zero or four leading to dosing errors, and the trap is using unsafe abbreviations.
Question 2011

A patient says they have “heartburn” after meals and points to the upper abdomen; which system should the CMA connect to this symptom for accurate intake documentation?

The gastrointestinal system, because reflux-related burning is typically GI in origin and the trap is documenting it as chest pain without clarifying associated GI triggers.
Question 2012

A patient with a history of asthma reports wheezing after exposure to perfume in the waiting room; which body system is primarily involved?

The respiratory system is primarily involved, because wheezing reflects lower airway narrowing and the trap is attributing it to cardiac causes without recognizing the airway symptom pattern.
Question 2013

A patient receives a new prescription for prednisone and asks why they were told to take it with food; what should the CMA say within scope?

Reinforce the instruction to take it with food as labeled and refer medication-effect questions to the provider, because food can reduce GI upset and the trap is giving detailed pharmacologic counseling beyond CMA scope.
Question 2014

The MAR shows a patient is due for a second dose today, but the patient says, “I already took it an hour ago at home”; what is the CMA’s best next step?

Hold the dose and immediately inform the provider for guidance, because double-dosing is a safety risk and the trap is administering based only on the schedule without verifying patient-reported dosing.
Question 2015

A provider order reads “give 0.5 mL IM,” but the vial concentration available in the room is different than what the patient previously received; what should the CMA do first?

Pause and verify the medication, concentration, and ordered dose with the provider/pharmacy before preparing it, because volume alone can be misleading and the trap is drawing up the same mL as last time.
Question 2016

A patient reports burning with urination and urinary frequency; which system should the CMA recognize as most likely involved when documenting the complaint for the provider?

The urinary (genitourinary) system, because dysuria and frequency commonly point to bladder/urinary tract involvement and the trap is documenting it as a GI complaint.
Question 2017

A patient taking an oral antibiotic says, “I feel better, so I stopped after two days”; what should the CMA do next?

Encourage the patient to contact the provider and follow the prescribed course unless instructed otherwise, because stopping early can lead to treatment failure and the trap is telling them to restart without provider guidance.
Question 2018

A patient reports “dizzy and nauseated since this morning,” but the CMA notes pale, clammy skin; when documenting, which are symptoms vs signs?

Document dizziness and nausea as symptoms (patient-reported) and pallor/diaphoresis as signs (observed), because a common trap is charting observations as symptoms, which reduces clinical clarity.
Question 2019

A patient asks the CMA to explain an EHR problem list term “HTN”; what should the CMA do?

Use plain language (“high blood pressure”) and confirm understanding, because HTN is a common abbreviation that affects comprehension and the trap is using more jargon that increases misunderstanding.
Question 2020

During intake, the provider’s order says “apply medication topically to the affected area,” and the patient asks if that means to swallow it; what should the CMA do next?

Clarify that “topical” means applied on the skin and verify the exact site/instructions in the order before giving directions, because a common trap is assuming the patient understands route terminology and giving unsafe administration advice.
Question 2021

A teen patient says they feel “lightheaded” and the CMA documents “syncope”; what is the best correction?

Document the patient’s exact symptom (“lightheaded”) and do not label it as syncope unless the patient actually fainted, because CMAs should avoid diagnostic terms and the trap is upcoding the complaint.
Question 2022

Question 2023

Clarify and expand “CP” as “chest pain” in documentation (per approved abbreviations/clinic standards), because a common trap is leaving ambiguous abbreviations that can mean different things in different contexts.
Question 2024

Question 2025

Question 2026

Question 2027

Question 2028

Question 2029

A patient says they have “heartburn” after meals and asks if it’s a heart problem; what is the CMA’s best response within scope?

Explain that “heartburn” commonly refers to acid reflux from the GI system and relay the concern to the provider for evaluation, because a trap is reassuring it’s “not cardiac” (diagnosing) or ignoring possible red flags.
Question 2030

Question 2031

While reviewing a medication list, the CMA sees “metoprolol” and the patient reports feeling unusually slow and tired; which body system effect should the CMA be most alert to?

Cardiovascular effects (slower heart rate/low blood pressure symptoms) should be flagged to the provider, because a trap is attributing it solely to “fatigue” without recognizing potential bradycardia/hypotension.
Question 2032

A patient complains of shortness of breath and wheezing after using a new cleaning spray at work; what system connection should the CMA recognize and communicate in the note?

Respiratory system involvement (bronchospasm pattern) should be highlighted, because a trap is documenting it as a skin/allergy-only issue and underemphasizing airway symptoms.
Question 2033

A prescription label reads “take 2 tablets BID,” and the patient asks how many tablets that is per day; what should the CMA answer?

It equals 4 tablets per day (2 tablets twice daily), because a common trap is interpreting BID as “once daily” or confusing it with TID/QID frequency.
Question 2034

The provider orders a medication “SL,” and the patient tries to chew it; what instruction should the CMA give?

Instruct the patient to place it under the tongue to dissolve (sublingual) and confirm they understand the route, because a trap is allowing chewing/swallowing which can reduce intended absorption/effect.

Question 2036

The provider e-prescribed a liquid antibiotic; the parent asks how to measure 7.5 mL at home; what is the CMA’s best instruction?

Instruct to use a marked oral syringe or dosing cup from the pharmacy (not a kitchen spoon) and confirm understanding, because household teaspoons are inaccurate and the trap is giving unsafe measurement advice.
