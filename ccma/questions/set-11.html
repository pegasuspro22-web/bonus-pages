Question 1001

A provider’s written order is “MSO4 2 mg IM now,” and the patient asks what it is; what should the CMA do first?

Stop and clarify the order with the provider before proceeding because “MSO4” is an error-prone abbreviation and the trap is administering without verifying the exact medication (morphine vs magnesium sulfate confusion).
Question 1002

A patient’s chart note includes “OD redness x3 days,” but the patient is pointing to the left eye—what should the CMA do next?

Ask the patient to confirm which eye is affected and document using “right/left” rather than OD/OS, because laterality errors are common and the trap is assuming OD/OS was used correctly.
Question 1003

A patient reports burning with urination and frequent small voids; which body system is most directly involved and what should the CMA do first in clinic workflow?

The urinary system is most involved; obtain a clean-catch urine specimen per protocol, because timely specimen collection supports evaluation and the trap is delaying until after the visit and missing the best sample.
Question 1004

A patient comes in for “acid reflux” and points to the epigastric area; which organ system is primarily involved and what is the CMA’s best next step during intake?

The gastrointestinal system; document location, timing, triggers, and associated symptoms (e.g., chest pain red flags) because accurate history guides the visit and the trap is charting it as “GERD diagnosis.”
Question 1005

A patient says, “I’ve had nausea all morning,” but the CMA observes the patient actively vomiting in the lobby; which is a symptom and which is a sign for documentation?

Nausea is a symptom and vomiting observed by staff is a sign, because symptoms are subjective reports and the trap is labeling patient statements as objective “signs.”
Question 1006

A walk-in patient says they feel “lightheaded” after starting a new “ACE” medication yesterday; what system-related effect should the CMA suspect and what is the best immediate action within scope?

Suspect cardiovascular/blood pressure effects and recheck vitals promptly, because ACE inhibitors can lower BP and the trap is dismissing it as anxiety without objective assessment.
Question 1007

During intake, the provider note says “c/o palpitations x2 days”; the patient points to their smartwatch heart rate log—what should the CMA document “c/o” as and why does it matter?

Document “complains of,” because it signals a patient-reported symptom (not an objective finding), and the trap is charting it as a “diagnosis” or “sign” the CMA didn’t measure.
Question 1008

A teen checks in for a sports physical and tells the CMA they are “Sx: dizzy” on the form; what is the best way for the CMA to interpret “Sx” before documenting?

Clarify with the patient and document as “symptoms” only if that’s what they mean, because “Sx” can be misread and the trap is copying ambiguous abbreviations into the record.
Question 1009

The provider orders an IM injection of 0.8 mL; the vial concentration is 100 mg/mL and the MAR lists dose in mg, not mL—what is the CMA’s safest first step?

Pause and verify the ordered dose in mg with the provider before calculating volume, because mismatched units drive dosing errors and the trap is converting from an incomplete or unclear order.
Question 1010

A provider verbal order is given over the phone for a medication dose; what is the CMA’s best action to prevent an error before documenting?

Perform a read-back of the medication name, dose, route, and timing before entering it, because read-back catches sound-alike errors and the trap is silently transcribing an unsafe verbal order.
Question 1011

A prescription label reads “Take 0.5 tablet twice daily,” but the patient says they take “half a pill once a day to make it last longer”; what should the CMA do next?

Educate to take exactly as labeled and notify the provider about nonadherence, because underdosing can be unsafe/ineffective and the trap is accepting the patient’s self-adjustment without escalation.
Question 1012

A patient with seasonal allergies says, “My throat feels tight,” and has audible wheezing; which system is most concerning and what should the CMA do first?

The respiratory system; alert the provider immediately and initiate emergency response per clinic protocol, because wheeze/tightness can indicate airway compromise and the trap is treating it as routine “allergy symptoms.”
Question 1013

A parent hands the CMA a liquid medication cup and says, “I give 1 teaspoon,” but the label is in mL; what is the best response?

Instruct to measure in mL using a marked oral syringe/cup and convert as needed (5 mL = 1 tsp), because household spoons are inaccurate and the trap is reinforcing unsafe measuring.
Question 1014

Question 1015

Stop and immediately notify the provider before administering anything, because anaphylaxis history is a high-risk contraindication and the trap is assuming “it’s probably fine” or proceeding to “double-check later.”
Question 1016

Question 1018

Question 1099

A patient on warfarin calls asking if they can start an OTC “NSAID for back pain”; what should the CMA do?

Instruct the patient to consult the provider/pharmacist before starting it and escalate the message, because NSAIDs increase bleeding risk with warfarin and the trap is casually approving OTC meds.
Question 1100

During medication history, a patient says they’re “allergic to penicillin” but can’t describe the reaction; what should the CMA do next?

Ask what happened when they took it (e.g., rash, hives, swelling, GI upset) and document the reaction details, because “allergy” often reflects side effects and vague entries are a common reconciliation trap.
